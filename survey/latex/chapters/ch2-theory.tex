\section{Theoretical Foundations and Taxonomy}\label{ch:theory}

\subsection{Introduction and Motivation}

Agent self-evolution---the capacity of an AI system to autonomously improve its own performance through iterative cycles of generation, evaluation, and adaptation---draws from four distinct but converging intellectual traditions. Evolutionary computation provides the foundational framework of ``generate--vary--select--retain'' for open-ended search over structured solution spaces. Self-reference and the theory of G\"odel machines contribute a recursive perspective in which a system reasons about and modifies its own architecture. Meta-learning supplies the machine learning machinery for ``learning how to learn,'' enabling agents to extract transferable strategies from past experience. Finally, reinforcement learning, online learning, and program synthesis offer formal mathematical characterizations of goals, policies, environments, evaluators, and update operators.

This chapter does not attempt to present a mature unified theory of agent self-evolution---the relevant literature is still in rapid formation. Rather, its goal is to abstract the common structural patterns underlying diverse methods and provide a conceptual coordinate system for the methodological taxonomy developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}. We begin by examining how evolutionary computation and large language models (LLMs) interact synergistically (Section~\ref{sec:theory-evo}), then turn to self-reference and G\"odel machines (Section~\ref{sec:theory-godel}), meta-learning frameworks (Section~\ref{sec:theory-meta}), and a unified mathematical formalization (Section~\ref{sec:theory-formal}). The centerpiece of the chapter is the Five Evolution Loops taxonomy (Section~\ref{sec:theory-five-loops}), which provides a principled classification of self-evolution mechanisms into five recurring structural patterns. We conclude with an analysis of cross-loop interactions (Section~\ref{sec:theory-cross-loop}), theoretical boundaries and open questions (Section~\ref{sec:theory-boundaries}), and a summary (Section~\ref{sec:theory-summary}).

\subsection{Evolutionary Computation Meets Large Language Models}\label{sec:theory-evo}

\subsubsection{Classical Evolutionary Computation}

The fundamental idea of evolutionary computation is to maintain a population or archive of candidate solutions within a search space, iteratively improving fitness through variation, recombination, and selection. Classical genetic algorithms, genetic programming, evolution strategies, novelty search, and quality-diversity algorithms all belong to this tradition. Their key advantage is that they do not require the objective function to be differentiable, nor do they require candidate solutions to have fixed-length representations---as long as candidates can be evaluated, search can proceed.

The search spaces encountered in agent self-evolution are precisely the kind where classical gradient-based methods fail. Prompts are discrete text sequences; tool chains are combinatorial structures; agent code consists of programs; memories are collections of natural language or vector entries; multi-agent topologies are graph structures; and world models and evaluators may themselves be composed of code or LLM prompts. The non-differentiable, discrete, and compositional nature of these spaces makes evolutionary computation a natural fit for describing the outer optimization loop of self-evolving agents.

\subsubsection{How LLMs Transform Evolutionary Search}

Large language models transform two critical components of the evolutionary loop. First, LLMs serve as powerful \emph{semantic variation operators}. Traditional mutation operators typically perform random bit-flips, Gaussian perturbations, or local rewrites, with no understanding of why a candidate failed or what improvement might look like. In contrast, LLMs can generate targeted modifications informed by failure analysis: adding a test-run step for a coding agent, rewriting the exploration strategy for a web agent, inserting self-verification for a reasoning agent, or designing debate or critic roles for a multi-agent system. The meta agent search in ADAS~\citep{adas2024}, the self-modification mechanism in DGM~\citep{dgm2025}, the code updates in SICA, and the algorithm candidate generation in AlphaEvolve~\citep{alphaevolve2025} all employ LLMs as high-level program mutation operators. Unlike random perturbation, LLM-based mutation carries strong semantic priors about program structure, functional intent, and common failure modes.

Second, LLMs can participate in \emph{evaluation and interpretation}. Systems such as Self-Rewarding Language Models~\citep{selfrewarding2024}, Meta-Rewarding, IterAlign, ACE, and EvolveR all use model-generated evaluations, principles, critiques, or policy summaries. This means that selection pressure no longer derives solely from hard performance metrics---it can also incorporate natural language preferences, process-level judgments, and qualitative feedback. The LLM-as-a-judge paradigm effectively creates a soft, adaptable fitness function that can be applied even when formal evaluators are unavailable.

\subsubsection{Five Design Dimensions of Agent Evolution}

From the evolutionary computation perspective, agent self-evolution involves at least five critical design dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Representation.} What constitutes an individual candidate? Possibilities include prompts, policies, memories, tool plans, Python code, workflow graphs, or model weights. The choice of representation fundamentally determines what can be varied and how. ADAS and DGM represent agents as Python programs; AlphaEvolve represents solutions as algorithmic code; FunSearch evolves single mathematical functions; OPRO operates on natural language prompts.

\item \textbf{Variation.} How are new candidates generated? Options range from random perturbation and LLM rewriting, to reflection-based text generation, text gradients from symbolic backpropagation, Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), reinforcement learning updates, or program patches. Agent Symbolic Learning~\citep{agentsymbolic2024} introduces ``language gradients'' that mimic backpropagation in natural language space; DGM uses LLM-proposed code modifications; AlphaEvolve combines breadth-first exploration (Gemini Flash) with depth-oriented refinement (Gemini Pro).

\item \textbf{Fitness.} How are candidates evaluated? Fitness can be defined by benchmark scores, unit test pass rates, environment rewards, LLM judge scores, human preferences, cost constraints, or safety rules. The choice of fitness function is arguably the most consequential design decision, as it determines the direction of evolutionary pressure. FunSearch and AlphaEvolve use programmatic evaluators; Reflexion uses binary task success; Self-Rewarding uses the model's own judgment.

\item \textbf{Selection.} Which candidates are retained? Options include keeping the single best, preserving novel solutions, maintaining quality-diversity archives, partitioning by task domain, or applying rollback-gated acceptance. AlphaEvolve employs MAP-Elites quality-diversity search, maintaining the best solution per behavioral niche; DGM maintains an open-ended, ever-growing archive; ADAS retains a history of past designs for in-context learning.

\item \textbf{Inheritance.} How is experience transmitted across generations? Mechanisms include weight updates, context accumulation, memory databases, code repositories, skill libraries, principle collections, or archive metadata. Voyager's skill library enables reuse across Minecraft tasks; ExpeL consolidates multi-task experience into transferable insights; ADAS discoveries transfer across domains and even across base models.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Challenges of the LLM--Evolution Combination}

The marriage of LLMs and evolutionary computation also introduces novel challenges. LLM-generated candidates carry strong distributional priors from training data---the search does not explore uniformly, and may inherit biases and stereotyped patterns present in the training corpus. When LLMs serve as judges (fitness functions), they are susceptible to length bias, position bias, and reward hacking~\citep{zheng2023judging, salewski2023context}. If the archive retains only high-scoring candidates, premature convergence is a risk; if it retains all candidates, computational costs and context pressure escalate rapidly.

DGM~\citep{dgm2025} addresses this by maintaining an ever-growing agent archive, using open-ended exploration to avoid local optima. ADAS~\citep{adas2024} leverages the history of past discoveries within the meta agent's context to compose novel agents. These two approaches illustrate a key insight: in agent evolution, the archive is not merely a log---it is an externalized repository of reusable search experience. How to manage, prune, index, and retrieve from this archive remains an open research question.

\subsection{Self-Reference, G\"odel Machines, and Recursive Self-Improvement}\label{sec:theory-godel}

\subsubsection{The G\"odel Machine Vision}

Self-reference is perhaps the most theoretically provocative element of agent self-evolution. The G\"odel machine, conceived by Schmidhuber~\citep{schmidhuber2003godel}, envisions a formally provably optimal self-improving system. The original formulation consists of a problem solver and a proof searcher: when the proof searcher finds that a particular self-modification would increase expected utility without violating global optimality guarantees, the system executes the modification. The significance of this framework lies in its transformation of ``recursive self-improvement'' from science fiction into a formal problem: how does a system represent itself, reason about the consequences of self-modification, and avoid situations where local improvements undermine long-term objectives?

The original G\"odel machine requires three components: (1) a formal representation of the system's own code and utility function, (2) a proof system capable of reasoning about the effects of code modifications, and (3) a guarantee mechanism ensuring that no executed modification can violate the system's global constraints. In theory, this yields a system that is provably optimal in the sense that no other self-improving system satisfying the same constraints can achieve higher expected cumulative reward.

\subsubsection{From Formal Proofs to Empirical Verification}

Modern LLM-based agents differ markedly from the original G\"odel machine vision. G\"odel Agent~\citep{godelagent2025}, DGM~\citep{dgm2025}, and SICA all borrow the self-referential idea, but they overwhelmingly substitute empirical evaluation for formal proof. G\"odel Agent allows an LLM to modify its own logic through runtime monkey patching---the LLM reads its source code, proposes specific patches, applies them at runtime, and validates the result. SICA enables a coding agent to autonomously edit its own source code to improve performance on SWE-Bench Verified. DGM maintains an archive of executable agent variants, allowing stronger coding agents to generate even stronger descendants through iterative code modification.

These systems demonstrate the feasibility of what we term \emph{practical G\"odel-ization}: rather than requiring the system to prove global optimality, it suffices that each variant is \emph{reliably} better in isolated evaluation. This relaxation dramatically expands the space of feasible self-modifications, but at the cost of formal guarantees. The shift from proof to empirical validation raises a central theoretical question: when formal proofs are unavailable, what level of empirical verification suffices to support self-modification?

\subsubsection{Safety Layers for Self-Modification}

If the evaluator provides insufficient coverage, the system may optimize for loopholes. If evaluation tasks diverge from the true objective, the system may succumb to Goodhart's law. If the agent can modify the evaluator itself or bypass tests, then the notion of ``improvement'' becomes meaningless. Empirical G\"odel agents must therefore decompose self-reference into several controlled layers:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Modifiable layer:} The components that may change---prompts, tools, code modules, or policies.
\item \textbf{Immutable evaluation layer:} The assessment procedures that must remain isolated from modification.
\item \textbf{Sandboxed execution layer:} The runtime environment that prevents side effects from escaping containment.
\item \textbf{Archive recording layer:} The lineage tracking that preserves full history and enables rollback.
\item \textbf{Human audit layer:} The oversight mechanism that inspects high-risk modifications.
\end{itemize}

Without these layered protections, self-reference easily degenerates into uncontrolled self-rewriting. The design of robust safety architectures for self-modifying systems remains a critical open problem.

\subsubsection{Capability Feedback and Recursive Structure}

Self-reference also involves the recursive structure of ``capability feedback.'' The key insight of DGM~\citep{dgm2025} is that a stronger coding agent can not only solve more external tasks, but also better improve its own code. Therefore, downstream task performance and self-modification capability form a positive feedback loop. This structure is strictly stronger than ordinary AutoML, because the search engine itself is also an object being searched. ADAS still retains a fixed meta agent (the LLM that proposes new agent designs), while DGM allows the improved agent to continue assuming responsibility for self-improvement.

This distinction corresponds to two theoretical models: the \emph{exogenous optimizer model}, where an external search process optimizes a fixed target, and the \emph{endogenous optimizer model}, where the system being optimized is simultaneously the optimizer. The former is easier to control and reason about; the latter is closer to true recursive self-improvement, but presents substantially greater challenges in safety and evaluation. The spectrum between these two extremes---from fixed external search to fully self-referential optimization---represents a key design axis for self-evolving agent systems.

\subsection{Meta-Learning and the Theory of Self-Improvement}\label{sec:theory-meta}

\subsubsection{Learning How to Learn in Agent Evolution}

Meta-learning, broadly construed, concerns the improvement of a learning system's own learning processes. In the context of agent self-evolution, meta-learning need not manifest as MAML-style gradient updates~\citep{finn2017maml}; it can also appear as the gradual optimization of experiential principles, contextual strategies, memory retrieval mechanisms, task curricula, and tool selection policies.

Reflexion~\citep{reflexion2023} compresses failed trajectories into natural language reflections, effectively storing first-order policy updates in context. ExPeL consolidates experience across multiple tasks into transferable insights. ICE (Investigate, Consolidate, Exploit) transforms task trajectories into streamlined workflows. ACE treats the context as an ever-evolving playbook. EvolveR organizes offline self-distillation, online interaction, and policy evolution into a coherent lifecycle. These systems extend ``learning'' from the weight space of neural networks to the space of external cognitive artifacts---memories, principles, skills, and procedures.

\subsubsection{A Three-Layer Policy Decomposition}

An LLM-based agent can be decomposed into three policy layers~\citep{selfevolve_survey2024}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Base layer} ($\theta$): The foundation model parameters, typically obtained through pre-training and post-training alignment. This layer is the most expensive to modify and is shared across all tasks.
\item \textbf{Behavioral interface} ($\varphi$): The editable behavioral configuration, including system prompts, tool descriptions, planner specifications, critic templates, code modules, and multi-agent topologies. This layer is task-specific and relatively inexpensive to modify.
\item \textbf{Experiential state} ($m$): The accumulated experience, including episodic memories, skill libraries, principle collections, failure reflections, world models, and archive metadata. This layer grows organically through interaction and is the most dynamic.
\end{itemize}

Traditional fine-tuning primarily updates $\theta$; prompt engineering updates $\varphi$; memory-based self-evolution updates $m$; systems like SICA, DGM, and ADAS simultaneously modify $\varphi$ and the coded structural components; RAGEN~\citep{ragen2025}, Absolute Zero~\citep{absolutezero2025}, and SPIRAL may update $\theta$ or policy parameters through reinforcement learning. The generalized policy of a self-evolving agent can be written as $\pi(a \mid s; \theta, \varphi, m)$, and self-evolution is the process of updating some subset of $(\theta, \varphi, m)$ under the drive of feedback $r$ or evaluation $e$.

\subsubsection{Task Distribution Learning}

A critical insight from meta-learning is the role of task distributions. A system that merely repeats trial-and-error on a single fixed task may achieve only local optimization; genuine self-evolution requires the extraction of cross-task strategies from a family of tasks. Voyager's skill library enables reuse across Minecraft exploration tasks; ExPeL demonstrates forward transfer of experience; ADAS reports that discovered agent designs transfer across domains and even across base models; ReasoningBank distills reasoning strategies from successful and failed samples for application to novel problems.

Formally, this can be expressed as minimizing expected loss over a task distribution $\mathcal{T}$: the system does not merely seek the optimal behavior for a single task $\tau$, but learns an update operator $U$ such that after a small number of interactions, it performs better on new tasks drawn from $\mathcal{T}$. The distinction between single-task optimization and distribution-level meta-learning is crucial for understanding the depth of self-evolutionary improvement.

\subsubsection{Self-Improvement as Online Learning}

Self-improvement can also be viewed through the lens of online learning. At each round $t$, the agent executes a trajectory $\tau_t$ in environment $\mathcal{E}$, the evaluator produces feedback $f_t$, and the updater $U$ generates a new state:
\begin{equation}
z_{t+1} = U(z_t, \tau_t, f_t), \quad z_t = (\theta_t, \varphi_t, m_t)
\end{equation}

If $\theta$ is fixed and only $m$ is updated, this constitutes \emph{memory-based self-evolution} (e.g., Reflexion, Voyager). If $\varphi$ is updated, it is \emph{prompt/architecture self-evolution} (e.g., ADAS, ACE). If $\theta$ is updated, it is \emph{training-based self-evolution} (e.g., Self-Rewarding via iterative DPO, RAGEN via StarPO). If the environment $\mathcal{E}$ or task generator is also updated, it represents \emph{self-curriculum} or \emph{self-play} (e.g., Absolute Zero, SPIN).

This unified framework facilitates comparison across methods: Reflexion's $U$ writes reflective memories; Self-Rewarding's $U$ performs iterative DPO; RAGEN's $U$ is trajectory-level RL; DGM's $U$ applies code patches with archive selection; ACE's $U$ incrementally restructures the contextual playbook. The nature of $U$---what it modifies, what feedback it requires, and what guarantees it provides---is the primary axis of differentiation among self-evolution approaches.

\subsection{A Unified Mathematical Formalization}\label{sec:theory-formal}

For unified description, this survey adopts the following formalization. Let the agent system state be $z = (\theta, c, g, m, \mathcal{A})$, where $\theta$ represents the base model or trainable parameters, $c$ represents context and prompts, $g$ represents tools, code, and control flow graphs, $m$ represents memories, skills, principles, and world models, and $\mathcal{A}$ represents the archive or historical candidate set. Given a task distribution $P(\tau)$, environment $\mathcal{E}$, evaluator $V$, and safety constraints $C$, the agent at round $t$ generates a behavioral trajectory $x_t \sim \pi_z(\cdot \mid \tau_t, \mathcal{E})$, receives feedback $y_t = V(x_t, \tau_t, \mathcal{E})$, and undergoes constraint checking $C(x_t, z_t)$. The updater $U$ produces a set of candidate states $\{z'_{t,k}\}$, and the selector $S$ chooses $z_{t+1}$ or adds multiple candidates to $\mathcal{A}$ based on quality, diversity, cost, and safety gates.

This process can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:update}
z_{t+1},\, \mathcal{A}_{t+1} = S\!\Big(\mathcal{A}_t \cup \{U_k(z_t, x_t, y_t)\}_{k=1}^K ;\; V,\, C,\, D\Big)
\end{equation}
where $D$ represents a diversity or novelty measure. Different methods are primarily distinguished by their definitions of $U$, $V$, $S$, and $D$.

Self-Refine's~\citep{selfrefine2023} $U$ is the same model rewriting its output based on self-generated feedback, with state changes occurring primarily in the text of a single sample. Reflexion's~\citep{reflexion2023} $U$ writes reflections into $m$. Self-Rewarding's $V$ is the model's own judge, and $U$ is DPO. RAGEN's~\citep{ragen2025} $V$ derives from multi-turn environment rewards, and $U$ is StarPO. ADAS's $U$ has the meta agent write new agent code, modifying $g$. DGM's $U$ has the current agent modify its own code, with $\mathcal{A}$ preserving the lineage. AlphaEvolve's $V$ is a programmatic evaluator, and $U$ generates algorithmic code. Memory-R1's $U$ learns when to write, update, delete, and retrieve from memory.

\subsubsection{Levels of Improvement}

The formalization requires distinguishing between levels of ``improvement'':

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Local improvement:} The score on $V$ increases on the current task set. Many systems demonstrate this level.
\item \textbf{Robust improvement:} Performance increases on an independent test set held out from training tasks.
\item \textbf{Transfer improvement:} Performance increases on a new task distribution or new base model. ADAS, DGM, Voyager, and ReasoningBank attempt to demonstrate this level.
\item \textbf{Open-ended improvement:} The archive continues to produce novel and useful stepping stones without convergence, a property related to open-endedness in evolutionary computation~\citep{stanley2017open}.
\item \textbf{Safe improvement:} Capability growth is not accompanied by increased constraint violations.
\end{itemize}

Many papers demonstrate only the first two levels. A critical direction for future theory is to develop rigorous distinctions among these levels; otherwise, ``self-improvement'' risks being over-interpreted from single-benchmark gains.

\subsubsection{Selection Pressure and Multi-Objective Optimization}

The evaluator $V$ determines the direction of evolutionary pressure. If $V$ is unit tests, the system will optimize for test passage; if $V$ is an LLM judge, it will optimize for judge preferences; if $V$ is environment reward, it will exploit reward structure loopholes; if $V$ is human preference, it may overfit to the annotation distribution. Goodhart's law---when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure---applies with particular force to self-evolving systems.

Therefore, self-evolution systems typically require multi-objective optimization: maximizing task reward $R$, minimizing computational cost $\mathrm{Cost}$, satisfying safety constraints $C$, maintaining diversity $D$, and controlling complexity $\Omega$. Formally, we seek $z$ that maximizes:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[R(z)] - \lambda\,\mathrm{Cost}(z) + \beta\,D(z) - \gamma\,\Omega(z)
\end{equation}
subject to $C(z) = \mathrm{true}$. In practice, exact optimization is typically infeasible, and systems rely on gating mechanisms, ablation studies, rollback procedures, and human review to approximate the multi-objective solution.

\subsection{Five Evolution Loops: A Taxonomy}\label{sec:theory-five-loops}

Having established the theoretical foundations, we now introduce the \emph{Five Evolution Loops} taxonomy---a principled classification of self-evolution mechanisms into five recurring structural patterns. Each loop represents a distinct feedback cycle through which an AI system can autonomously improve. While individual systems may implement multiple loops simultaneously, the taxonomy provides a clean decomposition that facilitates comparison and highlights design trade-offs.

\subsubsection{The Specification-to-Execution Loop}\label{sec:loop-spec}

\paragraph{Definition.}
The specification-to-execution loop transforms high-level natural language goals into executable machine learning or agent pipelines, evaluates their performance, and uses the evaluation to refine the specification interpretation or pipeline structure. This loop is the most direct realization of the ``describe what you want, get what works'' paradigm.

\paragraph{Formal Model.}
Let $\sigma$ denote a natural language specification, $\mathcal{P}(\sigma)$ the space of pipelines consistent with $\sigma$, and $V_p$ a pipeline evaluator. The loop seeks:
\begin{equation}
p^* = \argmax_{p \in \mathcal{P}(\sigma)} V_p(p, \mathcal{D}_{\text{eval}})
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_{\text{eval}}$ is an evaluation dataset. The updater $U_{\text{spec}}$ takes a failed pipeline $p_t$ and its evaluation $V_p(p_t)$ and produces a refined pipeline $p_{t+1}$, potentially also refining the specification interpretation $\hat{\sigma}_{t+1}$.

\paragraph{Representative Systems.}
AutoML-GPT~\citep{automlgpt2023} uses GPT as a natural language bridge, converting task cards, model cards, and data cards into data processing pipelines, model architectures, and hyperparameter optimization strategies. AutoML-Agent~\citep{automlagent2024} decomposes the full AutoML workflow into specialized agents for data retrieval, preprocessing, modeling, evaluation, and deployment, combining retrieval-augmented planning with multi-stage verification. ADAS~\citep{adas2024} pushes this loop further: the meta agent writes Python code defining complete agent architectures, evaluates them on benchmarks (ARC, GFootball), and stores successful designs in an archive. Crucially, ADAS demonstrates that discovered agent designs transfer across domains---agents designed on ARC perform well on GFootball---and even across base models---agents discovered with GPT-3.5 remain effective with GPT-4.

\paragraph{Benchmark Evidence.}
ADAS achieves performance exceeding hand-designed state-of-the-art agents on ARC and GFootball, with cross-domain transfer confirmed. AutoML-Agent demonstrates end-to-end pipeline generation on domain-specific datasets. AutoML-GPT shows competitive performance on standard AutoML benchmarks including tabular, vision, and NLP tasks.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The specification-to-execution loop faces several challenges. First, the quality of the generated pipeline is bounded by the LLM's understanding of the specification and the domain. Second, the evaluation may not capture all relevant quality dimensions (e.g., robustness, fairness, deployment efficiency). Third, most current systems automate single projects rather than accumulating cross-run experience. The gap between single-run automation and persistent evolution is precisely where self-evolution systems can differentiate.

\subsubsection{The Search Loop}\label{sec:loop-search}

\paragraph{Definition.}
The search loop explores a structured space of candidates---architectures, prompts, code, agent configurations, or hyperparameters---using feedback from evaluations to guide subsequent exploration. Unlike the specification-to-execution loop, which focuses on compiling a specification into an implementation, the search loop treats the entire candidate space as its object and seeks to discover high-performing configurations that may not be directly derivable from any single specification.

\paragraph{Formal Model.}
Given a search space $\mathcal{S}$, an evaluation function $V_s: \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$, and a history $H_t = \{(s_i, V_s(s_i))\}_{i=1}^{t}$, the search loop generates:
\begin{equation}
s_{t+1} = \mathrm{LLM}(H_t, \text{strategy})
\end{equation}
where the LLM proposes new candidates informed by the history of prior attempts and their evaluations. The key design choices are the representation of $\mathcal{S}$, the exploration--exploitation strategy, and the diversity mechanism.

\paragraph{Representative Systems.}
OPRO~\citep{opro2023} (Optimization by PROmpting) treats natural language prompts as the optimization variable: given a history of prompt--score pairs, the LLM proposes new prompts that achieve higher scores on the target task. EvoPrompting~\citep{evoprompting2023} uses LLMs as evolutionary operators for code-level neural architecture search, evaluating candidate GNN architectures on algorithmic reasoning tasks. LLMatic~\citep{llmatic2023} combines LLM-guided search with quality-diversity optimization, maintaining diverse high-performing architectures rather than converging to a single optimum. AlphaEvolve~\citep{alphaevolve2025} employs a dual-model search strategy: Gemini Flash provides breadth through rapid exploration of many candidates, while Gemini Pro provides depth through focused refinement of promising solutions.

\paragraph{Benchmark Evidence.}
OPRO demonstrates improvements on GSM8K and BIG-Bench Hard through automatic prompt optimization. EvoPrompting achieves competitive GNN architectures on algorithmic reasoning benchmarks. AlphaEvolve's search discovers the first improvement over Strassen's matrix multiplication algorithm in 56 years---achieving 48 scalar multiplications for $4\times4$ complex matrix multiplication---and recovers 0.7\% of worldwide Google compute through improved data center scheduling.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Search effectiveness is bounded by the LLM's ability to propose meaningful variations. The search may inherit distributional biases from the LLM's training data. Computational cost scales with the number of evaluations, which can be prohibitive for expensive benchmark tasks. Quality-diversity methods require careful tuning of behavioral descriptors and niche boundaries.

\subsubsection{The Evaluator Loop}\label{sec:loop-evaluator}

\paragraph{Definition.}
The evaluator loop focuses on the verification and scoring component of self-evolution. It encompasses systems that co-evolve the evaluator alongside the generator, generate their own test cases, or use automated verification tools to provide feedback. The quality of the evaluator is often the binding constraint on the quality of evolution---a system can only evolve toward what its evaluator can measure.

\paragraph{Formal Model.}
Let $G$ denote a generator and $V_e$ an evaluator. The evaluator loop co-optimizes:
\begin{align}
\max_G \; &V_e(G(x), x) \\
\max_{V_e} \; &\text{alignment}(V_e, V^*_{\text{true}})
\end{align}
where $V^*_{\text{true}}$ represents the (typically inaccessible) true evaluation function. The second optimization ensures that the evaluator remains aligned with genuine quality rather than exploitable proxies.

\paragraph{Representative Systems.}
FunSearch~\citep{funsearch2024} (published in Nature) pairs an LLM with a programmatic evaluator to evolve mathematical functions, demonstrating that LLM-generated programs can surpass human-designed algorithms when coupled with rigorous automated verification. ReVeal~\citep{reveal2026} explicitly co-optimizes self-verification alongside code generation using TAPO (Turn-level Adaptive Policy Optimization), achieving per-turn credit assignment and enabling 20+ turns of self-evolution at inference despite training on only 3 turns. SelfEvolve~\citep{selfevolve2023} generates its own API documentation (self-generated knowledge) and uses sandbox execution with error traceback feedback for iterative self-debugging. Self-Debug~\citep{selfdebug2023} uses execution results, test outcomes, and natural language debugging information as first-class self-repair signals.

\paragraph{Benchmark Evidence.}
FunSearch produces the first new construction for the cap set problem that surpasses established mathematical bounds, and discovers new sorting algorithms. ReVeal outperforms DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B on LiveCodeBench. SelfEvolve improves HumanEval pass@1 from 74.39\% to 85.98\%, DS-1000 from 49.4 to 57.2 (+15.8\% relative), and TransCoder accuracy from 88.3\% to 90.4\%.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The evaluator loop's fundamental challenge is the alignment between the proxy evaluator and true quality. If the evaluator has gaps, evolution will exploit them. If the evaluator is an LLM judge, it may exhibit length bias, verbosity preference, or positional effects. If the evaluator is a unit test suite, evolution may produce solutions that pass tests without being genuinely correct. ReVeal's approach of co-evolving the verifier represents a promising direction but adds training complexity. AlphaEvolve's reliance on ``well-defined evaluation functions'' limits its applicability to domains with automated verifiers.

\subsubsection{The Reflection Loop}\label{sec:loop-reflection}

\paragraph{Definition.}
The reflection loop transforms failures into memories, feedback, or revised candidate generators. Rather than directly modifying the agent's architecture or weights, the reflection loop enriches the agent's experiential context, enabling it to avoid past mistakes and replicate successful strategies. This loop operates primarily in the space of natural language representations---verbal feedback, structured critiques, and experiential principles.

\paragraph{Formal Model.}
At each episode $e$, the agent generates an action trajectory, receives an evaluation $r_e$, and produces a reflection $f_e$:
\begin{align}
a_e &= M_a(\text{task}, M_{1:e-1}) \\
r_e &= M_e(a_e, \text{task}) \\
f_e &= M_r(a_e, r_e, \text{task}) \\
M_e &= M_{e-1} \cup \{f_e\}
\end{align}
where $M_a$, $M_e$, $M_r$ are the actor, evaluator, and self-reflection models respectively, and $M_{1:e}$ is the growing memory of past reflections. The reflection $f_e$ functions as a ``verbal gradient''---a natural language description of what went wrong and how to correct it.

\paragraph{Representative Systems.}
Reflexion~\citep{reflexion2023} pioneered verbal reinforcement learning: the agent stores linguistic feedback describing what it attempted, why it failed, and what to do differently. This memory buffer acts as a persistent context that guides future behavior without any weight updates. Self-Refine~\citep{selfrefine2023} performs iterative generate--critique--refine cycles within a single inference session: the same model generates an output, critiques it against a rubric, and refines it, requiring no external feedback or training. STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner)~\citep{star2022} generates rationales, filters correct ones, and fine-tunes on successful reasoning chains, bootstrapping reasoning capability from the model's own outputs. Agent Symbolic Learning~\citep{agentsymbolic2024} treats agents as symbolic networks where learnable ``weights'' are prompts, tools, and pipeline compositions, performing backpropagation and gradient descent in natural language space through ``language gradients'' that describe how to improve each component.

\paragraph{Benchmark Evidence.}
Reflexion achieves 91.0\% pass@1 on HumanEval using GPT-3.5, surpassing GPT-4's 80.1\% zero-shot performance, and improves AlfWorld success from 75\% to 97\%. Self-Refine delivers approximately 20\% average improvement across 7 diverse tasks: +28\% on code optimization, +11pp on math reasoning (56\% to 67\%), +16pp on dialogue response quality, and +13pp on constrained generation compliance. Agent Symbolic Learning improves MATH accuracy from 56.0\% to 60.7\% (GPT-4 backbone), HotPotQA F1 from 36.2 to 39.1, and HumanEval pass@1 from 68.3\% to 73.2\%.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The reflection loop's effectiveness is bounded by the quality of the base model's self-critique capability. Self-Refine may get trapped in local optima where the model's own critique cannot identify fundamental flaws. Reflexion's memory buffer grows unbounded, eventually encountering context window limits. Agent Symbolic Learning requires GPT-4-level backbones for gradient computation. No existing system provides convergence guarantees for language-space optimization. The ``verbal gradient'' may not faithfully represent the true improvement direction, particularly for complex multi-component systems.

\subsubsection{The Population Loop}\label{sec:loop-population}

\paragraph{Definition.}
The population loop maintains multiple candidate solutions or agents simultaneously, applying selection, variation, recombination, and specialization across the population. Unlike single-agent refinement, the population loop explicitly preserves diversity and enables competitive or cooperative dynamics among candidates. This loop draws directly from evolutionary computation's population-based optimization paradigm.

\paragraph{Formal Model.}
Maintain a population $P_t = \{p_1, \ldots, p_N\}$ with fitness scores $\{f_1, \ldots, f_N\}$. At each generation:
\begin{align}
p' &= \text{LLM-Vary}(\text{Sample}(P_t, f_{1:N}, D)) \\
f' &= V(p') \\
P_{t+1} &= \text{Select}(P_t \cup \{p'\}, f_{1:N} \cup \{f'\}, D)
\end{align}
where $D$ enforces diversity (e.g., MAP-Elites behavioral niches), and the variation operator is an LLM rather than random mutation. The key distinguishing features are the explicit maintenance of multiple candidates and the use of diversity-preserving selection.

\paragraph{Representative Systems.}
AlphaEvolve~\citep{alphaevolve2025} employs MAP-Elites quality-diversity search, maintaining a map of high-performing solutions across behavioral dimensions (niches) and keeping the best solution per niche. The dual-model architecture (Gemini Flash for breadth, Gemini Pro for depth) is specifically designed to balance exploration and exploitation across the population. DGM~\citep{dgm2025} maintains an open-ended, ever-growing archive of agent variants with diversity-biased sampling, allowing the archive to serve as a repository of evolutionary stepping stones rather than converging to a single optimum. SPIN~\citep{spin2024} implements self-play fine-tuning, where a model trains against its own previous iteration---the ``population'' consists of the current and previous model versions. Absolute Zero~\citep{absolutezero2025} creates a self-play curriculum where a single model simultaneously proposes tasks and solves them, with the code executor providing verification.

\paragraph{Benchmark Evidence.}
AlphaEvolve achieves a historic result on matrix multiplication (48 multiplications for $4\times4$ complex matrices, first improvement over Strassen in 56 years), solves 75\% of 50+ mathematical competition problems at or above SOTA, recovers 0.7\% of worldwide Google compute, and achieves 23\% speedup on FlashAttention kernel tiling. DGM improves SWE-bench Verified from 20.0\% to 50.0\% and Polyglot from 14.2\% to 30.7\%, discovering novel agent structures not imagined by human designers. SPIN outperforms DPO and other RLHF methods on multiple benchmarks by training models through self-play. Absolute Zero achieves SOTA at the 7B parameter scale on coding benchmarks with zero external training data, and demonstrates emergent mathematical reasoning from code self-play alone.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Population-based methods are computationally expensive, requiring multiple evaluations per generation. Archive management---how to prune, index, and retrieve from growing populations---remains an unsolved engineering challenge. MAP-Elites requires domain-specific behavioral descriptors that may not generalize. Open-ended archives may accumulate noise and redundancy. The tension between exploration (preserving diversity) and exploitation (refining the best) is a perennial challenge with no general solution.

\subsection{Cross-Loop Interactions and Composite Systems}\label{sec:theory-cross-loop}

The Five Evolution Loops are not isolated mechanisms---real systems almost invariably combine multiple loops. Understanding these interactions is essential for designing effective self-evolution architectures and for analyzing the behavior of existing systems. We identify four primary patterns of cross-loop interaction.

\subsubsection{Search + Evaluator: Verified Search}

The combination of search and evaluator loops is perhaps the most natural and powerful pairing. The search loop proposes candidates, and the evaluator loop provides rigorous verification. FunSearch~\citep{funsearch2024} exemplifies this pattern: an LLM generates candidate mathematical functions, and a deterministic evaluator verifies their correctness and measures their quality. AlphaEvolve~\citep{alphaevolve2025} extends this pattern with a population-level component: Gemini Flash proposes many candidate solutions (search), a programmatic evaluator scores them (evaluator), and MAP-Elites maintains diversity across the solution space (population). The resulting three-loop combination achieves results that no single loop could produce in isolation.

The critical design parameter in search--evaluator combinations is the \emph{fidelity gap}: the difference between the proxy evaluator $V$ and the true quality measure $V^*$. When the fidelity gap is small (as in formal mathematical verification), evolution can proceed confidently. When the gap is large (as in open-ended creative tasks), the system risks optimizing for the proxy rather than true quality. ReVeal~\citep{reveal2026} addresses this by co-evolving the verifier, effectively reducing the fidelity gap over time.

\subsubsection{Reflection + Evaluator: Self-Correcting Agents}

The reflection loop provides the ``why did I fail?'' signal, while the evaluator loop provides the ``did I succeed?'' signal. Reflexion~\citep{reflexion2023} combines these two loops: a binary evaluator determines task success, and a self-reflection model generates natural language explanations of failure that are stored in memory for future attempts. The combination is remarkably effective: GPT-3.5 with Reflexion achieves 91\% on HumanEval, surpassing GPT-4's 80\% zero-shot performance. This demonstrates that even a simple binary evaluator, when coupled with rich reflective feedback, can drive substantial improvement.

SelfEvolve~\citep{selfevolve2023} adds a specification-to-execution component: the system generates its own API documentation (specification), uses sandbox execution as the evaluator, and feeds error tracebacks back as reflective feedback. The three-loop combination achieves significant improvements on code generation benchmarks.

\subsubsection{Population + Reflection: Evolutionary Experience Accumulation}

DGM~\citep{dgm2025} combines population-level diversity with reflective self-modification. The population loop maintains an open-ended archive of agent variants, while the self-modification mechanism (driven by the agent's own LLM-based reflection on its failures) serves as the variation operator. The population's diversity ensures that self-modification does not converge prematurely, while the reflective variation operator ensures that modifications are semantically meaningful rather than random.

Absolute Zero~\citep{absolutezero2025} creates a different population-level dynamic: the ``population'' consists of the model's own behavioral variants across a self-generated task curriculum. The model simultaneously proposes tasks (search), solves them (specification-to-execution), and verifies solutions (evaluator), with self-play providing the population-level competitive pressure. The emergent mathematical reasoning from code self-play alone suggests that population-level dynamics can produce qualitatively novel capabilities.

\subsubsection{All Five Loops: The Self-Evolution Thesis}

The central thesis of this survey is that genuine self-evolution requires the integration of all five loops. Systems that implement only a subset---for example, Self-Refine (reflection only) or OPRO (search only)---demonstrate improvement within their operational scope but fail to achieve open-ended, robust, transferable self-improvement. The most capable systems in the current landscape---AlphaEvolve, DGM, Absolute Zero---each combine three or more loops.

We hypothesize that the five loops form a natural hierarchy: the specification-to-execution loop provides the task grounding; the search loop provides the exploration mechanism; the evaluator loop provides the selection pressure; the reflection loop provides the learning signal; and the population loop provides the diversity and open-endedness. A complete self-evolution system would integrate all five, with each loop reinforcing the others in a virtuous cycle of generation, evaluation, reflection, and discovery.

\subsection{Theoretical Boundaries and Open Questions}\label{sec:theory-boundaries}

Despite the rapid progress surveyed in this chapter, several fundamental theoretical limitations of agent self-evolution must be acknowledged.

\subsubsection{Self-Reward Hallucination}

Without an external or independent evaluator, self-rewarding systems may fall into what we term \emph{self-consistent hallucination}: the evaluator and generator share the same biases and blind spots, creating a closed loop of mutual affirmation. The model judges its own outputs as high-quality because they conform to its internal preferences, not because they are genuinely superior. This is the self-evolution analog of the ``echo chamber'' effect in social systems. RAGEN's identification of the ``Echo Trap''---where agents learn to repeat previous outputs rather than genuinely reasoning---represents a specific manifestation of this broader concern.

\subsubsection{Benchmark Overfitting}

If the task distribution is too narrow, the system may learn benchmark-specific hacks rather than generalizable improvements. A self-evolving code agent that optimizes purely for HumanEval pass rates may develop strategies that exploit the specific structure of HumanEval problems without improving its general coding ability. This concern is amplified by the observation that many self-evolution papers evaluate on a small set of standard benchmarks (HumanEval, GSM8K, MMLU, AlfWorld), raising questions about the breadth of demonstrated improvement.

\subsubsection{Unbounded Modification Scope}

If the range of modifiable components is too broad and constraints are insufficient, the system may damage its own evaluation, logging, or safety boundaries. An agent that can modify its own test harness, for example, can achieve ``perfect'' scores by rewriting the tests rather than fixing the code. This is not a hypothetical concern: DGM's self-modifying agents and G\"odel Agent's runtime monkey patching both operate without formal safety proofs, relying instead on empirical validation and sandboxing.

\subsubsection{Archive Degradation}

Long-running self-evolution systems face the challenge of archive management. If the archive lacks proper curation, long-term memory accumulates noise, conflicts, and outdated entries. Context window limitations constrain how much historical experience can be brought to bear on new problems. The tension between comprehensive archiving (preserving all potentially useful stepping stones) and efficient retrieval (finding the most relevant experience for the current task) remains unresolved.

\subsubsection{Base Model Capability Ceiling}

Theoretical analysis suggests that agent self-evolution cannot create capabilities that are entirely absent from the base model's distribution. LLM-generated variations explore the neighborhood of the model's existing knowledge; they cannot reliably leap to genuinely novel paradigms. Self-evolution amplifies and recombines existing capabilities, but does not transcend the fundamental limitations of the underlying model. This ``capability ceiling'' hypothesis, while not formally proven, is consistent with the empirical observation that self-evolution systems built on stronger base models (GPT-4, Gemini) consistently outperform those built on weaker models, even when the evolution mechanisms are identical.

\subsubsection{Toward a Formal Theory of Self-Evolution}

The theoretical analysis in this chapter points toward several directions for a more rigorous formal treatment of agent self-evolution:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Convergence analysis:} Under what conditions does the self-evolution process converge to a fixed point? When does it diverge? What is the relationship between convergence and the expressiveness of the variation operator?
\item \textbf{Sample complexity bounds:} How many evaluation rounds are needed to achieve a given level of improvement? How does this scale with the complexity of the search space and the quality of the evaluator?
\item \textbf{Transfer guarantees:} Under what conditions do improvements on one task distribution transfer to another? Can we formalize the conditions under which ADAS-style cross-domain transfer occurs?
\item \textbf{Safety invariants:} What formal properties must the evaluator and safety constraints satisfy to guarantee that self-modification cannot produce harmful behaviors? How can we formally verify that the immutable evaluation layer remains intact?
\item \textbf{Open-endedness metrics:} How can we measure whether a self-evolution system is making genuinely novel discoveries versus recombining known strategies? What is the right formal definition of ``open-ended improvement'' in this context?
\end{enumerate}

These questions remain largely open and represent fertile ground for theoretical contributions that could bridge the current gap between empirical demonstrations and formal understanding.

\subsection{Chapter Summary}\label{sec:theory-summary}

The theoretical landscape of agent self-evolution can be summarized as follows. Large language models provide semantic generation and reasoning capabilities. Evolutionary computation provides the framework for open-ended candidate search. Self-reference and G\"odel machines provide the aspiration and architectural patterns for recursive self-improvement. Meta-learning provides the machinery for cross-task experience reuse. Reinforcement learning and online learning provide feedback-driven update mechanisms. Software engineering contributes testing, sandboxing, version control, and rollback mechanisms.

The Five Evolution Loops taxonomy---specification-to-execution, search, evaluator, reflection, and population---provides a principled decomposition of the diverse mechanisms found in the literature. Real systems combine these loops in characteristic patterns: search+evaluator for verified discovery, reflection+evaluator for self-correction, population+reflection for evolutionary experience accumulation, and the integration of all five loops for genuine open-ended self-evolution.

The field has not yet achieved a mature unified theory comparable to, say, the theory of supervised learning. However, a set of stable questions has emerged: what is the object of change, is the evaluator reliable, how should the archive be managed, can improvements transfer, and how can safety constraints be made tamper-proof. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} will develop the methodological taxonomy within this theoretical framework, examining specific systems and their instantiation of the five evolution loops.
