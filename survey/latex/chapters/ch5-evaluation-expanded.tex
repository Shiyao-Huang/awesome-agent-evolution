\section{Evaluation Systems and Benchmarks}\label{ch:evaluation}

\subsection{Chapter Overview}\label{sec:ch5-overview}

The central tension in agent self-evolution research lies between ``the system claims to have become stronger'' and ``how do we verify that it has genuinely improved.'' Evaluation is not merely an experimental section in papers---it functions as the \textbf{selection pressure} and \textbf{safety gate} of the entire self-evolution loop. A self-evolving agent that is evaluated against a flawed benchmark may optimize for the wrong objective, and an agent evaluated without safety checks may evolve in dangerous directions.

This chapter provides a comprehensive analysis of evaluation systems for agent self-evolution. We organize the evaluation landscape into five layers (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-layers}), from fully automated programmatic verification to human-involved safety gatekeeping. We then analyze major benchmarks across four domains---code generation, mathematical reasoning, agent tasks, and open-ended exploration---and introduce process-oriented metrics that capture the dynamics of self-evolution rather than just final performance. Finally, we present a star quality analysis of open-source projects to assess community health and detect hype-driven versus organic growth.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}\label{sec:ch5-methodology}

\subsubsection{The Evaluation Challenge in Self-Evolution}

Evaluating self-evolving agents presents unique challenges that distinguish it from standard machine learning evaluation:

\textbf{Non-Stationarity}: The agent's behavior changes over time as it evolves. A benchmark score at time $t$ does not guarantee the same score at time $t+1$, even on identical inputs. This means that traditional single-point evaluation is insufficient; we need \emph{longitudinal evaluation} that tracks performance over the entire evolution trajectory.

\textbf{Evaluator Quality}: When the agent serves as its own evaluator (as in Self-Rewarding, Meta-Rewarding), the evaluation signal may be corrupted by the same biases that affect generation. RAGEN's Echo Trap discovery~\citep{ragen2025} demonstrated that seemingly effective training processes can produce spurious improvement signals---the agent learns to repeat its own outputs rather than genuinely reasoning. This raises the fundamental question: \emph{how can we verify that self-reported improvements are real?}

\textbf{Goodhart's Law}: When a benchmark becomes the optimization target, the benchmark ceases to be a good measure of the true objective. This is especially dangerous in self-evolution systems that can iterate millions of times---they have ample opportunity to discover and exploit benchmark-specific shortcuts that do not generalize.

\textbf{Distribution Shift}: As the agent evolves, its output distribution shifts. Evaluators calibrated on early-stage outputs may become miscalibrated for later-stage outputs, leading to inaccurate quality assessments precisely when the stakes are highest.

\textbf{Cost-Quality Tradeoff}: More reliable evaluation (human annotation, real-world deployment) is more expensive; cheaper evaluation (automated benchmarks, LLM-as-judge) is less reliable. Self-evolution systems must navigate this tradeoff continuously.

\subsubsection{Formal Evaluation Framework}

We formalize the evaluation of self-evolving agents using the following framework:

\begin{definition}[Self-Evolution Evaluation Protocol]
Let the agent at evolution step $t$ be $\pi_t$, the evaluation benchmark be $\mathcal{B} = \{(x_i, y_i^*, m_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ where $x_i$ is the input, $y_i^*$ is the reference output, and $m_i$ is the metric. The evaluation produces a score vector:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_t = \left[\text{score}(\pi_t, x_1), \ldots, \text{score}(\pi_t, x_N)\right]
\label{eq:eval-score-vector}
\end{equation}
The \textbf{evolution gain} at step $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_t = f(\mathbf{s}_t) - f(\mathbf{s}_{t-1})
\label{eq:evolution-gain}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is the aggregation function (mean, median, etc.).
The \textbf{cumulative evolution gain} over $T$ steps is:
\begin{equation}
G_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Delta_t = f(\mathbf{s}_T) - f(\mathbf{s}_0)
\label{eq:cumulative-gain}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Dimensions}

Self-evolution methods should be evaluated along six orthogonal dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Target Performance} ($P$): Absolute performance on target tasks, measured by task-specific metrics (e.g., pass@1 for code, accuracy for math).

\item \textbf{Improvement Magnitude} ($\Delta$): The magnitude of improvement relative to baseline:
\begin{equation}
\Delta = P_{\mathrm{evolved}} - P_{\mathrm{baseline}}
\label{eq:improvement-magnitude}
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Improvement Efficiency} ($\eta$): Improvement per unit of computational resources:
\begin{equation}
\eta = \frac{\Delta}{C_{\mathrm{total}}}
\label{eq:improvement-efficiency}
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{total}}$ is the total cost (FLOPS, API calls, GPU hours).

\item \textbf{Generalization Ratio} ($G$): Whether improvements transfer to out-of-distribution tasks:
\begin{equation}
G = \frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{OOD}}}{\Delta_{\mathrm{ID}}}
\label{eq:generalization-ratio}
\end{equation}
where ID = in-domain, OOD = out-of-domain. $G > 0$ indicates positive transfer; $G > 1$ indicates that OOD improvement exceeds ID improvement.

\item \textbf{Stability} ($S$): Variance of improvement across multiple independent runs:
\begin{equation}
S = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\Delta)}{\max(\Delta)^2}
\label{eq:stability-metric}
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Safety}: Whether harmful behaviors emerge during evolution, measured by permission violation rate, reward hacking rate, and human audit findings.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Common Evaluation Pitfalls}

Based on our analysis of the literature, we identify five common evaluation pitfalls:

\textbf{Baseline Selection Bias}: Choosing weak baselines to exaggerate improvements. For example, Reflexion's 91.0\% on HumanEval is reported relative to a GPT-3.5 baseline of 67.0\%; against GPT-4's 80.1\%, the improvement shrinks to 10.9pp. When comparing against GPT-4 + few-shot, the gap narrows further.

\textbf{Evaluation Domain Shift}: Reporting only in-domain performance overestimates generalization. Absolute Zero's practice of testing mathematical reasoning after code self-play training is commendable---it explicitly tests cross-domain transfer rather than assuming it.

\textbf{Randomness Neglect}: LLM output stochasticity means single-run performance can vary significantly. At least 3--5 independent runs should be reported with mean and standard deviation.

\textbf{Improvement Attribution}: When combining multiple techniques (e.g., Reflexion + STaR + DGM), it is difficult to attribute improvement to specific components. Ablation studies are essential but often omitted due to computational cost.

\textbf{Echo Trap}: The phenomenon discovered by RAGEN~\citep{ragen2025} where training processes appear effective but are merely teaching the model to repeat its own outputs. All self-evolution evaluations should include echo detection:
\begin{equation}
\text{Echo}(t_t, t_{t-1}) = \mathbb{1}\left[\cos_{\mathrm{sim}}(t_t, t_{t-1}) > \tau_{\mathrm{echo}}\right]
\label{eq:echo-detection}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Key Challenges for Web Benchmarks}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dynamic environments}: Web pages change over time, making reproducibility difficult
\item \textbf{Cost}: Each evaluation requires actual web interaction or simulation
\item \textbf{Evaluation complexity}: Success criteria may be ambiguous (e.g., ``find the best hotel'' depends on subjective criteria)
\item \textbf{Contamination}: Low risk, since web environments are dynamic and hard to memorize
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Open-Ended Benchmarks}

Voyager uses unique item discovery count and tech tree unlock speed to measure lifelong learning in Minecraft. AlphaEvolve uses mathematical correctness as a verifiable metric for algorithm discovery.

\textbf{Open-ended evaluation is fundamentally different from standard benchmarking}:
\begin{itemize}
\item There is no fixed test set---the system must demonstrate continuous improvement over time
\item Success is measured by the discovery of novel, useful artifacts rather than scores on predefined tasks
\item Evaluation may require expert judgment (e.g., whether a mathematical proof is correct)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Coverage Gaps}

Our analysis reveals significant coverage gaps in the current evaluation landscape:

\textbf{Memory Evolution Blind Spot}: No widely-adopted benchmark systematically evaluates memory evolution capabilities. Memory-R1 and AriadneMem report task-specific improvements but lack standardized comparison protocols.

\textbf{Safety Evaluation Gap}: No benchmark specifically tests whether self-evolution processes produce unsafe behaviors. Safety is typically assessed qualitatively rather than quantitatively.

\textbf{Cost-Normalized Comparison}: Most papers report only final performance, not performance-per-dollar. This makes it impossible to compare methods that achieve similar performance at vastly different costs (e.g., Reflexion's $O(n \times k)$ API calls vs.\ STaR's single-pass fine-tuning).

\textbf{Long-Term Evolution Tracking}: No standard protocol for tracking agent performance over hundreds or thousands of evolution steps. Most papers report results after a fixed number of iterations, missing potential late-stage degradation or improvement.

\subsubsection{Propagation Chain Analysis}

\paragraph{AutoGPT --- Viral Hype Propagation}

AutoGPT's star growth followed a textbook viral pattern: 0 to 50K in 3 days (March 30--April 2, 2023), driven by Elon Musk's retweet and YouTube KOL demonstrations. Over 90\% of stargazers had no subsequent GitHub activity. This represents the ``hype-driven'' growth mode---rapid initial adoption followed by rapid decay.

DSPy grew from 0 to 25K over approximately 12 months, driven primarily by paper citations, conference presentations, and genuine developer adoption. Its Stars/Contributor ratio of $\sim$80 (within the healthy range) reflects sustained community engagement.

\paragraph{Devika --- Me-Too Project}

Devika achieved 20K stars in 5 days by positioning itself as ``the open-source alternative to Devin.'' Within 3 months, activity dropped to near-zero. PR merge rate was only 38\%. This is a textbook ``me-too'' project: exploiting a competitor's hype without delivering sustainable value.

\subsubsection{Three Growth Paradigms}

Our analysis identifies three distinct growth paradigms:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Hype-Driven} (AutoGPT, Devika): Explosive growth ($<$7 days to 1K), rapid decay, low star quality, high Stars/Contributor ratio. These projects generate enormous initial attention but fail to retain developers.

\item \textbf{Steady Growth} (CrewAI, LangGraph): Moderate growth (30--60 days to 1K), sustained activity, healthy community metrics. These projects build value incrementally through consistent development and community engagement.

\item \textbf{Academic-Driven} (DSPy, SWE-Agent): Slow growth (60+ days to 1K), highest quality scores, paper citation support. These projects are undervalued by star count but deliver the most reliable research and engineering value.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Process-Oriented Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:ch5-process}

Beyond static benchmark scores, self-evolution systems require process-oriented metrics that capture the dynamics of evolution itself.

\subsubsection{Iteration Gain Curves}

The trajectory of improvement over evolution steps reveals important qualitative properties:

\textbf{Monotonic Improvement}: Each evolution step produces a net positive gain ($\Delta_t > 0$ for all $t$). This is the ideal but rarely achieved in practice.

\textbf{Plateau-and-Breakthrough}: Long periods of no improvement followed by sudden jumps. This pattern is common in architecture search methods (ADAS, DGM) where a structural innovation unlocks cascading improvements.

\textbf{Diminishing Returns}: Improvement magnitude decreases over time ($\Delta_t < \Delta_{t-1}$). This suggests convergence toward a performance ceiling.

\textbf{Oscillation}: Performance fluctuates up and down, indicating unstable evolution dynamics. This may result from evaluator noise, mode collapse, or reward hacking.

\textbf{Regression}: Performance degrades over time ($\Delta_t < 0$ for multiple consecutive steps). This indicates catastrophic forgetting or destructive self-modification.

\subsubsection{Archive Diversity Metrics}

For methods maintaining an archive (DGM, AlphaEvolve), archive diversity is a critical metric:

\begin{definition}[Archive Diversity]
Given archive $\mathcal{A} = \{a_1, \ldots, a_n\}$ with behavior descriptors $b(a_i)$, the archive diversity is:
\begin{equation}
D(\mathcal{A}) = \frac{|\{b(a_i) : a_i \in \mathcal{A}\}|}{|\mathcal{B}|}
\label{eq:archive-diversity}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is the space of possible behavior descriptors. A well-maintained archive should have $D(\mathcal{A})$ increasing over time, indicating coverage of qualitatively different solutions.
\end{definition}

\subsubsection{Transfer Metrics}

Cross-domain transfer is measured by the generalization ratio (Eq.~\ref{eq:generalization-ratio}). Notable transfer results include:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Code $\to$ Math}: Absolute Zero achieves competitive MATH performance with only code self-play training, suggesting transfer of logical reasoning skills.
\item \textbf{ARC $\to$ GFootball}: ADAS discovers agent designs on ARC that transfer to GFootball, demonstrating architectural invariance.
\item \textbf{GPT-3.5 $\to$ GPT-4}: ADAS-discovered agents remain effective when the underlying model is upgraded, suggesting that the discovered structures capture general principles rather than model-specific quirks.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Recommended Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:ch5-protocol}

Based on our analysis, we recommend the following evaluation protocol for self-evolution research:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Multi-Benchmark Evaluation}: Report results on at least two benchmarks from different categories (e.g., code + math, reasoning + interaction). This guards against benchmark-specific overfitting.

\item \textbf{Multi-Seed Runs}: Conduct at least 3--5 independent runs with different random seeds, reporting mean and standard deviation. This accounts for the inherent stochasticity of LLM-based evolution.

\item \textbf{Cross-Domain Testing}: Explicitly test generalization outside the training domain. Report the generalization ratio $G$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:generalization-ratio}).

\item \textbf{Ablation Studies}: Remove components one by one to determine each component's contribution. For computationally expensive methods (DGM, AlphaEvolve), at least partial ablations should be reported.

\item \textbf{Efficiency Reporting}: Report total computational cost including: total API calls, token consumption, GPU hours, wall-clock time, and cost-per-improvement-point.

\item \textbf{Iteration Gain Curves}: Plot performance over evolution steps to reveal convergence patterns, plateaus, and potential regressions.

\item \textbf{Echo Detection}: Include echo detection metrics (Eq.~\ref{eq:echo-detection}) to verify that improvements reflect genuine capability gains rather than output repetition.

\item \textbf{Safety Audit}: For self-modifying code methods, report: permission violation rate, code review findings, rollback frequency, and any emergent behaviors not explicitly programmed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Chapter Summary}\label{sec:ch5-summary}

This chapter has provided a comprehensive analysis of evaluation systems for agent self-evolution across five dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Evaluation Methodology} (\S\ref{sec:ch5-methodology}): We formalized the evaluation challenge and identified six evaluation dimensions (target performance, improvement magnitude, improvement efficiency, generalization, stability, safety) and five common pitfalls (baseline bias, domain shift, randomness neglect, attribution difficulty, echo trap).

\item \textbf{Code Generation Benchmarks} (\S\ref{sec:ch5-code}): HumanEval remains the most widely used benchmark, with Reflexion achieving 91.0\% pass@1. SWE-Bench provides the most realistic evaluation, with DGM achieving 50.0\% verified. DS-1000 fills the data science code niche.

\item \textbf{Mathematical Reasoning Benchmarks} (\S\ref{sec:ch5-math}): GSM8K and MATH are standard for evaluating reasoning improvements. Absolute Zero's cross-domain transfer from code to math is a particularly noteworthy result.

\item \textbf{Agent Task Benchmarks} (\S\ref{sec:ch5-agent}): HotPotQA, ALFWorld, WebArena, and open-ended environments like Minecraft test multi-step reasoning and interaction capabilities.

\item \textbf{Star Quality Analysis} (\S\ref{sec:ch5-star}): Star count does not correlate with project quality. The Stars/Contributor ratio is the most reliable hype detection metric. Three growth paradigms exist: hype-driven, steady, and academic-driven.
\end{enumerate}

We recommend a standardized evaluation protocol (\S\ref{sec:ch5-protocol}) that includes multi-benchmark evaluation, multi-seed runs, cross-domain testing, ablation studies, efficiency reporting, iteration gain curves, echo detection, and safety audits. Adopting this protocol would significantly improve the comparability and reliability of self-evolution research.
