\section{Self-Improvement Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The capacity for self-improvement is the most fundamental form of self-evolution in artificial intelligence systems. In this section, we provide a comprehensive taxonomy and analysis of methods through which large language models (LLMs) and language agents improve their own performance without explicit human intervention at every step. We organize these methods along two principal axes---\textbf{inference-time self-improvement}, where improvement occurs within a bounded number of interaction rounds without modifying model weights, and \textbf{training-time self-improvement}, where the model's parameters are iteratively refined using self-generated data. This distinction is not merely organizational; it carries deep implications for computational cost, the persistence and generality of learned improvements, the safety profile of the resulting system, and the composability of self-improvement with other capabilities. As we demonstrate below, the most impactful recent systems increasingly combine both paradigms, and the boundary between them is becoming progressively blurred.

We begin in Section~\ref{sec:methods-overview} with a systematic five-dimensional comparison of the inference--training divide. Sections~\ref{sec:inference-time} and~\ref{sec:training-time} then present detailed analyses of inference-time and training-time methods respectively, including formal algorithmic descriptions, quantitative results, and principled discussions of limitations. Section~\ref{sec:methods-comparison} provides a comprehensive cross-method comparison, and Section~\ref{sec:emerging-methods} surveys emerging hybrid approaches. We conclude with a summary of key principles and open problems in Section~\ref{sec:methods-summary}.


\subsection{Overview: The Inference--Training Divide}
\label{sec:methods-overview}

The distinction between inference-time and training-time self-improvement is one of the most consequential architectural choices in the design of self-evolving AI systems. Rather than treating this as a binary classification, we analyze it along five dimensions that collectively determine the practical applicability, scalability, and risk profile of each approach. These dimensions---cost, persistence, generalization, safety, and composability---are not independent; they interact in complex ways that we unpack throughout this section.


\subsubsection{Five-Dimensional Comparison}

\paragraph{Cost.}
Inference-time methods incur a marginal cost per query: each additional refinement step consumes additional compute (in the form of forward passes through the model) but requires no upfront investment in data curation, reward modeling, or gradient computation. The cost scales linearly with the number of refinement iterations $T$ and is proportional to the model's parameter count during inference. Training-time methods, by contrast, impose a significant fixed cost---the generation of candidate outputs, the construction of filtering or preference data, and the optimization of model parameters via gradient descent---but this cost is amortized over all subsequent queries. The cost profile of training-time methods is therefore $\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{gen}} \cdot C_{\text{gen}} + N_{\text{train}} \cdot C_{\text{train}})$, where $N_{\text{gen}}$ is the number of generated candidates, $C_{\text{gen}}$ is the per-candidate generation cost, $N_{\text{train}}$ is the number of training steps, and $C_{\text{train}}$ is the per-step training cost. For deployment scenarios with high query volume, training-time methods are generally more economical after the initial investment is recouped. Conversely, for research prototyping or low-throughput applications, the zero-setup cost of inference-time methods makes them the more practical choice.

\paragraph{Persistence.}
Inference-time improvements are ephemeral: they vanish at the end of each query and must be reconstructed from scratch for subsequent queries. The model's weights remain unchanged, and no accumulated knowledge persists across episodes unless explicitly stored in external memory (as in Reflexion's linguistic memory buffer). Training-time improvements, by contrast, are baked into the model's parameters and persist across all future queries without additional computational overhead. This persistence has a dual character: it ensures that improvements are always available, but it also means that errors or biases introduced during training are similarly persistent and may require explicit correction. The persistence dimension thus introduces a fundamental asymmetry between positive and negative modifications---good improvements accumulate freely, but so do harmful ones.

\paragraph{Generalization.}
Inference-time methods tend to improve performance on the specific instance being processed but do not, by themselves, produce generalizable knowledge that transfers to new instances. The refinement loop operates on a single input $x$ and produces an improved output $y_T$ for that input alone. Training-time methods, through the inductive biases of gradient descent and the diversity of the training data, produce improvements that generalize across the data distribution. A model fine-tuned on self-generated mathematical reasoning chains will typically improve not only on problems similar to those seen during training but also on related problems that share structural features. This generalization, however, is bounded by the quality and diversity of the self-generated data, and a well-known failure mode is distributional collapse, where the model's output distribution narrows around a limited set of patterns. The risk of collapse is particularly acute in iterative self-training, where each round of training is conditioned on the model's own outputs from the previous round.

\paragraph{Safety.}
The safety profiles of the two paradigms differ substantially. Inference-time methods are inherently more constrained: the model's base capabilities are fixed, and self-improvement operates within the envelope of what the model can already produce. While this limits the magnitude of improvement, it also limits the magnitude of potential harm---a model cannot self-refine its way to capabilities it fundamentally lacks. Training-time methods can produce more dramatic capability shifts, including the acquisition of behaviors not present in the original model, which raises concerns about reward hacking, distributional shift, and the alignment of the improved model with intended objectives. Constitutional AI~\citep{bai2022constitutional} was specifically designed to address these safety concerns by using principled self-critique as the basis for alignment. The safety dimension is particularly consequential for self-evolving systems because, as we discuss in subsequent sections, the most capable self-evolving systems tend to be those that modify their own parameters, precisely the regime where safety guarantees are hardest to establish.

\paragraph{Composability.}
Inference-time methods are highly composable: they can be stacked, interleaved, and applied selectively without modifying the underlying model. One can apply Self-Refine after Reflexion, or combine Self-Debug with Agent Symbolic Learning, without concern for parameter interference. Training-time methods are less composable in a strict sense because each fine-tuning step modifies the model's parameters, potentially overwriting or degrading improvements from prior training. However, training-time methods compose naturally with inference-time methods: a model trained via STaR or SPIN can subsequently benefit from Self-Refine at inference time, and the improvements are typically multiplicative rather than substitutive. The emerging hybrid methods discussed in Section~\ref{sec:emerging-methods} exploit this composability deliberately, training models to be better at inference-time self-correction and then using inference-time self-correction to generate higher-quality training data for the next round.


\subsubsection{Formal Framework}

We formalize the distinction as follows. Let $M_\theta$ denote a language model parameterized by weights $\theta$. Given an input $x$, the model produces an output $y \sim M_\theta(\cdot | x)$. An inference-time self-improvement method applies a transformation $\mathcal{T}$ to the model's output process without modifying $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
    y^{*} = \mathcal{T}(M_\theta, x, \mathcal{F})
    \label{eq:inference-improvement}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ represents the feedback signal (which may be self-generated, externally provided, or derived from execution). The transformation $\mathcal{T}$ is typically iterative, with $T$ rounds of refinement, and the cost is $T$ times the cost of a single forward pass.

A training-time self-improvement method, by contrast, modifies the model's parameters:
\begin{equation}
    \theta^{*} = \theta + \Delta\theta \quad \text{where} \quad \Delta\theta = \mathrm{Optimize}(\theta, \mathcal{D}_{\text{self}})
    \label{eq:training-improvement}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}$ is a dataset generated by the model itself (possibly filtered, ranked, or otherwise processed). The improvement persists: subsequent queries use $\theta^{*}$ rather than $\theta$, and the cost of improvement is amortized across all future queries.

The key insight is that these two formalisms are not mutually exclusive. A system may apply inference-time improvement $\mathcal{T}$ to a model that has already been improved via training-time methods, yielding a composed improvement that combines the generalization of parameter updates with the instance-specific adaptability of inference-time refinement. We can express this composition as:
\begin{equation}
    y^{*} = \mathcal{T}(M_{\theta^{*}}, x, \mathcal{F})
    \label{eq:composed-improvement}
\end{equation}
The emerging methods in Section~\ref{sec:emerging-methods} explicitly design for this composition, training models to be better at the inference-time self-correction process that is then applied during deployment.


\subsection{Inference-Time Self-Improvement}
\label{sec:inference-time}

Inference-time self-improvement methods enable a model to produce better outputs than its initial generation through iterative refinement, reflection, or debugging---all without modifying the model's parameters. These methods treat the LLM as a fixed function and improve outputs through structured interaction protocols. We present four representative methods that span the design space of inference-time self-improvement, from simple iterative refinement to structured textual backpropagation. Each method introduces progressively richer feedback mechanisms, from pure self-critique to execution traces to textual gradients.


\subsubsection{Self-Refine: Iterative Refinement with Self-Feedback}
\label{sec:self-refine}

Self-Refine~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine} introduces a minimal yet powerful framework: a single LLM iteratively generates, critiques, and refines its own output. The method requires no external feedback, no reinforcement learning, and no weight updates. Despite its simplicity, Self-Refine establishes the foundational paradigm upon which many subsequent methods build.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
The Self-Refine loop consists of three stages applied iteratively. We present it formally as Algorithm~\ref{alg:self-refine}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Self-Refine~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine}}
\label{alg:self-refine}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Input $x$, instructions $\text{INST}$, feedback rubric $\text{RUBRIC}$, maximum iterations $T$
\ENSURE Refined output $y^{*}$
\STATE $y_0 \leftarrow \mathrm{LLM}(x, \text{INST})$ \hfill \textcolor{gray}{// GENERATE}
\FOR{$t = 0, 1, \ldots, T-1$}
    \STATE $\mathrm{fb}_t \leftarrow \mathrm{LLM}(y_t, \text{RUBRIC})$ \hfill \textcolor{gray}{// CRITIQUE}
    \IF{$\mathrm{fb}_t$ indicates no improvement needed}
        \STATE \textbf{break}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $y_{t+1} \leftarrow \mathrm{LLM}(y_t, \mathrm{fb}_t, \text{INST})$ \hfill \textcolor{gray}{// REFINE}
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $y^{*} = y_{\min(t, T)}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Formally, the three stages are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GENERATE}: The model produces an initial output $y_0 = \mathrm{LLM}(x, \text{instruction})$ given input $x$ and task instructions.
    \item \textbf{CRITIQUE}: The model analyzes its own output and produces structured feedback: $\mathrm{fb}_t = \mathrm{LLM}(y_t, \text{rubric})$, where the rubric defines task-specific evaluation criteria such as correctness, completeness, coherence, and style.
    \item \textbf{REFINE}: The model improves the output based on the critique: $y_{t+1} = \mathrm{LLM}(y_t, \mathrm{fb}_t, \text{instruction})$.
\end{enumerate}

The loop terminates when the critique indicates no further improvements are needed or a maximum iteration count $T$ is reached. A critical design choice is that the \emph{same model} serves all three roles---generator, critic, and refiner---making Self-Refine applicable to any sufficiently capable LLM without additional training or specialized modules.

The refinement process can be understood as a form of fixed-point iteration in output space. Under idealized conditions, the sequence $y_0, y_1, y_2, \ldots$ converges to an output $y^{*}$ that is locally optimal with respect to the model's internal evaluation function. In practice, convergence is not guaranteed, and the method may oscillate or converge to a local optimum that is superior to the initial output but not globally optimal. The relationship between Self-Refine and fixed-point iteration suggests that the choice of rubric plays a role analogous to the contraction mapping in classical fixed-point theory: a well-designed rubric ensures that each refinement step moves the output closer to a desirable fixed point.

\paragraph{Results.}
Self-Refine achieves approximately 20\% average improvement across seven diverse tasks~\citep{madaan2023selfrefine}. On code optimization, it achieves a 28\% improvement; on math reasoning, accuracy rises from 56\% to 67\% (+11 percentage points); on sentiment reversal, accuracy increases from 74\% to 86\% (+12pp); on dialogue response generation, win rate rises from 52\% to 68\% (+16pp); on constrained generation, compliance increases from 65\% to 78\% (+13pp); on acrostic poetry, quality improves from 3.2 to 3.8 on a 5-point scale (+0.6); and on code readability, score improves from 3.5 to 4.1 (+0.6). These improvements are achieved with $T \leq 3$ refinement iterations in most configurations.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Self-Refine is bounded by the quality of the initial generation: if the model's first attempt is fundamentally flawed, self-critique may fail to identify the core issue. The method can also converge to local optima, where iterative refinement makes superficial improvements without addressing deeper structural problems. Each iteration increases compute cost linearly, and the method may ``refine away'' creative or diverse outputs, producing increasingly generic responses. Perhaps most fundamentally, Self-Refine lacks any mechanism for persistence: improvements do not accumulate across queries, and the same mistakes may be repeated on similar inputs.

A deeper concern is the phenomenon of \emph{compounding errors}. If the critique at step $t$ is incorrect---that is, if it identifies a non-existent flaw or suggests a harmful modification---the refined output $y_{t+1}$ may be worse than $y_t$. Over multiple iterations, these erroneous critiques can compound, leading to outputs that are substantially worse than the initial generation. This is particularly acute in domains where the model's self-evaluation capability is weak, such as creative writing or open-ended reasoning tasks where there is no clear ground truth.

\paragraph{The Self-Correction Paradox.}
An important empirical finding, documented across multiple studies, is that naive self-correction---simply instructing a model to ``check your answer'' or ``improve your response''---often \emph{degrades} performance rather than improving it. The model, lacking access to new information about the correctness of its output, may ``correct'' correct answers to incorrect ones, or introduce new errors while attempting to fix existing ones. Self-Refine overcomes this paradox partially by providing structured rubrics for self-critique, but the fundamental challenge remains: without external grounding, the model is operating on the basis of its own internal assessment, which may be unreliable. This paradox is a recurring theme in self-improvement research and motivates the introduction of external feedback signals in subsequent methods.

\paragraph{Practical Considerations.}
The choice of rubric is critical to Self-Refine's effectiveness. A well-designed rubric provides concrete, actionable evaluation criteria that guide the model's self-critique toward genuine deficiencies in the output. A poorly designed rubric, by contrast, may lead the model to focus on superficial aspects (grammar, formatting) while neglecting deeper issues (logical errors, factual inaccuracies). In practice, effective rubrics tend to be domain-specific and to encode the evaluation criteria that a human expert would apply. The optimal number of refinement iterations $T$ also varies by task: tasks with clear, binary success criteria (e.g., code compilation, mathematical correctness) benefit from additional iterations, while tasks with subjective quality criteria (e.g., creative writing quality) may see diminishing or negative returns beyond $T = 2$.


\subsubsection{Reflexion: Verbal Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:reflexion}

Reflexion~\citep{shinn2023reflexion} extends the self-improvement paradigm by introducing persistent linguistic memory that accumulates across episodes. Rather than refining a single output, Reflexion learns from failed attempts on a task to improve performance on subsequent attempts. The key insight is that natural language can serve as a medium for storing and transmitting experiential knowledge, analogous to how parameter gradients store and transmit learning signals in traditional optimization. This ``verbal reinforcement learning'' approach circumvents the need for differentiable parameters and instead stores experience in a format that is interpretable, composable, and transferable.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
Reflexion employs a three-component architecture:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Actor} ($M_a$): Generates actions or outputs given the task and memory context.
    \item \textbf{Evaluator} ($M_e$): Scores the actor's output, producing a binary reward $r_e \in \{0, 1\}$ or a scalar reward.
    \item \textbf{Self-Reflection} ($M_r$): Generates verbal feedback explaining what went wrong and how to improve.
\end{itemize}

The Reflexion loop operates across episodes $e = 1, 2, \ldots, E$ according to the following equations:
\begin{align}
    a_e &= M_a(\text{task}, \mathcal{M}_{1:e-1}) \label{eq:reflexion-actor} \\
    r_e &= M_e(a_e, \text{task}) \label{eq:reflexion-eval} \\
    f_e &= M_r(a_e, r_e, \text{task}) \quad \text{if } r_e \text{ indicates failure} \label{eq:reflexion-reflect} \\
    \mathcal{M}_e &= \mathcal{M}_{e-1} \cup \{f_e\} \label{eq:reflexion-memory}
\end{align}

The key innovation is the memory buffer $\mathcal{M}$, which stores linguistic feedback across episodes. Each entry describes what the agent tried, why it failed, and what to do differently. This acts as a ``verbal gradient''---natural language guidance that replaces the role of parameter gradients in traditional reinforcement learning. The accumulated reflections provide increasingly specific and actionable guidance, enabling the agent to avoid repeating past mistakes and to build on successful strategies.

The evaluator $M_e$ can be implemented in several ways: as a deterministic program (e.g., a unit test suite for code tasks), as a separate LLM prompted to evaluate output quality, or as the same LLM acting in a different role. The choice of evaluator has significant implications for the quality and reliability of the self-improvement process, a theme that recurs throughout this section. When the evaluator is a deterministic program, the feedback loop is grounded in objective correctness; when the evaluator is another LLM, the feedback inherits the biases and limitations of that model.

\paragraph{Results.}
Reflexion achieves 91\% pass@1 on HumanEval using GPT-3.5, surpassing GPT-4's 80\% in the zero-shot setting~\citep{shinn2023reflexion}. This result is remarkable because it demonstrates that a smaller, less capable model can outperform a larger, more capable model through structured self-improvement. On AlfWorld (interactive household environments), Reflexion achieves 97\% success versus 75\% for the ReAct baseline~\citep{yao2022react}, representing a 22 percentage point improvement. On the MBPP code generation benchmark, significant improvements are similarly observed.

The method demonstrates clear scaling behavior along two axes. First, more reflection episodes yield better performance, with diminishing returns typically setting in after 3--5 episodes. Second, specific, actionable feedback substantially outperforms generic feedback (e.g., ``try a different approach''), confirming that the quality of linguistic memory is a critical determinant of improvement magnitude. Cross-task transfer effects are observed: feedback from similar tasks provides partial benefit, suggesting that the linguistic memory captures generalizable problem-solving strategies rather than merely memorizing task-specific solutions. This transfer is particularly noteworthy because it suggests that verbal gradients, unlike parameter gradients, can be shared and reused across related tasks without additional training.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Reflexion requires a scalar or binary evaluation signal, which may not be available for all tasks. In open-ended generation tasks (e.g., creative writing, open-domain dialogue), there may be no reliable automatic evaluator, and the quality of self-evaluation becomes a bottleneck. The memory buffer grows unbounded, eventually exceeding the model's context window and requiring truncation or summarization strategies that may discard valuable information. The quality of self-reflection depends critically on the underlying LLM's capability, and there is no theoretical framework for convergence guarantees.

A particularly concerning failure mode is \emph{hallucinated reflections}: the model may generate plausible-sounding but incorrect explanations for why a particular approach failed. These hallucinated reflections, when stored in memory, can actively mislead the agent on subsequent episodes, degrading performance rather than improving it. This is an instance of the broader challenge of self-reference in self-improvement systems---the system's ability to improve is bounded by its ability to accurately diagnose its own failures. The hallucinated reflection problem is analogous to the ``noisy gradient'' problem in traditional optimization, where gradient noise can prevent convergence or cause the optimizer to diverge.


\subsubsection{Self-Debug: Execution-Guided Self-Repair}
\label{sec:self-debug}

Self-Debug~\citep{chen2024selfdebug} specializes self-improvement to the code domain by incorporating execution feedback as an external verification signal. Unlike Self-Refine and Reflexion, which rely on the model's own assessment of output quality, Self-Debug grounds its improvement process in the deterministic feedback provided by a code interpreter. The key innovation is ``rubber duck debugging''---the model explains its own code line by line, often discovering errors during the explanation process itself. This name derives from the software engineering practice of explaining code to an inanimate object (a rubber duck) as a debugging technique; the act of articulation often surfaces assumptions and errors that remain invisible during casual inspection.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
The Self-Debug loop operates as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GENERATE}: $p_0 = \mathrm{LLM}(x, \text{prompt})$---the model generates an initial program given the problem specification $x$.
    \item \textbf{EXECUTE}: $f_t = \mathrm{Execute}(p_t, \text{test\_cases})$---the program is executed against available test cases, producing execution traces, error messages, and pass/fail signals.
    \item \textbf{EXPLAIN}: $e_t = \mathrm{LLM}(p_t, \text{``explain this code''})$---the model explains its own code, step by step. This rubber duck debugging step often surfaces logical errors that the model can identify during explanation even when it failed to avoid them during generation.
    \item \textbf{DEBUG}: $p_{t+1} = \mathrm{LLM}(p_t, f_t, e_t, \text{debug\_prompt})$---the model repairs the program using the execution feedback and its own explanation.
\end{enumerate}

The method supports three levels of feedback granularity: (1)~Simple Feedback provides only a pass/fail signal with no detailed information; (2)~Unit Test Feedback provides the input/output pairs from failed test cases, enabling the model to understand which specific inputs trigger failures; and (3)~Code Explanation provides the model's own line-by-line explanation, which requires no external execution environment and can be applied even when test infrastructure is unavailable. The three levels form a hierarchy of information richness, and the model's debugging performance improves monotonically with the richness of available feedback.

\paragraph{Results.}
On Spider (text-to-SQL), Self-Debug with code explanation achieves up to 9\% improvement on the hardest difficulty level, demonstrating that the explanation step adds value beyond what execution feedback alone provides. On TransCoder (C++ to Python translation) and MBPP (Python generation), up to 12\% accuracy improvement is observed. Self-Debug is far more sample-efficient than best-of-$K$ sampling: it matches the performance of baselines that generate 10$\times$ more candidates, because the iterative debugging process provides targeted, informative feedback that random sampling cannot replicate.

\paragraph{Significance.}
Self-Debug bridges inference-time and execution-time improvement by leveraging a deterministic external evaluator (the code interpreter). This paradigm---generate, execute, diagnose, repair---recurs throughout the evolutionary code systems analyzed in subsequent sections of this survey, including FunSearch~\citep{chen2024funsearch} and AlphaEvolve~\citep{novikov2025alphaevolve}. The key insight is that execution feedback provides a form of objective grounding that is absent from purely self-referential methods: the code either compiles and passes tests, or it does not. This binary signal, while coarse, is remarkably effective at guiding the self-improvement process because it is noiseless---it never provides a false positive (passing a test that should have failed) and only rarely provides a false negative (failing a test that should have passed, which occurs only in the case of test suite errors).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The effectiveness of Self-Debug is fundamentally constrained by the quality and coverage of available test cases. If the test suite is incomplete---that is, if it does not exercise all edge cases or failure modes---the model may produce code that passes all available tests but fails on untested inputs. This is a form of overfitting to the test suite, and it is exacerbated when the model has access to the test cases during the debugging process, as it may learn to satisfy the specific test cases rather than implementing the intended algorithm correctly. This limitation is shared by all test-based evaluation methods and is a specific instance of the broader evaluation bottleneck that constrains all self-improvement systems.

Additionally, the explanation step, while often helpful, can also mislead. If the model generates an incorrect explanation of its own code, the debugging step may introduce new errors while attempting to align the code with the incorrect explanation. This limitation mirrors the broader challenge of relying on self-assessment for improvement: the system's ability to improve is bounded by its ability to accurately understand its own outputs.


\subsubsection{Agent Symbolic Learning: Language Backpropagation}
\label{sec:symbolic-learning}

Agent Symbolic Learning~\citep{zhou2024symbolic} represents a significant conceptual advance: it treats an LLM-based agent as a \emph{symbolic network} whose ``weights'' are natural language prompts, tool descriptions, and pipeline compositions, and then applies an analog of backpropagation and gradient descent entirely in text space. This framework bridges the inference--training divide by performing structured optimization without modifying neural network parameters, instead optimizing the symbolic configuration of the agent.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
An agent is parameterized as a computational graph with three types of learnable nodes: \textbf{Prompt nodes} ($\mathcal{P}$) for system prompts and instructions, \textbf{Tool nodes} ($\mathcal{T}$) for function definitions, and \textbf{Pipeline nodes} ($\mathcal{I}, \mathcal{O}$) for input/output connections between agent components.

The framework operates in four stages, mirroring the forward pass, loss computation, backpropagation, and weight update of neural network training:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Forward Pass}: Execute the agent on a task to produce a trajectory $\tau = \{(a_1, o_1), (a_2, o_2), \ldots, (a_n, o_n)\}$, where $a_i$ are actions and $o_i$ are observations.
    \item \textbf{Language Loss}: Evaluate the trajectory using an LLM-based loss function: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{lang}} = \mathrm{LLM}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{loss}}(\tau))$, where $\mathcal{P}_{\text{loss}}$ is a prompt template that instructs the LLM to assess trajectory quality.
    \item \textbf{Language Gradient Backpropagation}: For each layer $n$ from output to input, compute a textual gradient:
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla_{\text{lang}}^n = \mathrm{LLM}\left(\mathcal{P}_{\text{gradient}}\left(\nabla_{\text{lang}}^{n+1}, \mathcal{I}_n, \mathcal{O}_n, \mathcal{P}_n, \mathcal{T}_n, \mathcal{L}_{\text{lang}}\right)\right)
        \label{eq:symbolic-gradient}
    \end{equation}
    This process propagates improvement signals backward through the agent's computational graph, from the output layer (where the loss is computed) to the input layer (where the initial prompts and tool definitions reside).
    \item \textbf{Weight Update}: Apply textual updates to the agent's ``weights'': $\Delta\mathcal{P}$ (prompt updates), $\Delta\mathcal{T}$ (tool updates), and $\Delta\mathcal{I}, \Delta\mathcal{O}$ (pipeline updates). These updates are generated by the LLM and applied directly to the agent's configuration.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Results.}
On HotPotQA (multi-hop question answering), Agent Symbolic Learning achieves F1 = 39.1 and EM = 25.6, outperforming both vanilla agents (F1 = 36.2) and DSPy~\citep{khattab2023dspy} (F1 = 37.4). On MATH with GPT-4 backbone, accuracy reaches 60.7\% versus 56.0\% for baseline agents and 48.4\% for DSPy. On HumanEval, 73.2\% pass@1 is achieved versus 68.3\% baseline. For software development tasks (scored 1--4 on executability), Agent Symbolic Learning achieves 3.8 versus 2.4 for baseline agents and 1.6 for standard GPT configurations. On creative writing tasks, it achieves 7.4 versus 6.8 for Tree of Thought~\citep{yao2023treeofthoughts}.

\paragraph{Significance.}
Agent Symbolic Learning is the first framework to apply structured optimization---analogous to neural network training---to the full architecture of an LLM agent. It demonstrates that agent ``weights'' (prompts, tools, pipelines) can be systematically improved through a principled learning procedure. The textual backpropagation mechanism is particularly notable because it provides a general-purpose optimization framework that can be applied to any agent architecture, regardless of its specific structure. This establishes a foundation for the more radical self-modifying systems discussed in subsequent sections of this survey. The analogy to neural network training also provides a useful conceptual vocabulary for understanding and analyzing self-improvement in agent systems.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The quality of textual gradients depends on the LLM's ability to perform accurate credit assignment across the agent's computational graph---a task that is challenging even in the neural network setting, where gradients are computed exactly. In the textual domain, gradient computation is approximate and may be unreliable for complex multi-step agents. Additionally, the framework assumes that the agent's architecture can be decomposed into discrete, optimizable components, which may not be the case for all agent designs. The ``learning rate'' in text space (how aggressively to apply textual updates) is also an unexplored hyperparameter: too aggressive updates may destabilize the agent, while too conservative updates may yield insufficient improvement.

Several patterns emerge from this comparison. First, the introduction of \textbf{external grounding}---whether through execution feedback (Self-Debug), evaluator scores (Reflexion), or structured optimization objectives (Agent Symbolic Learning)---consistently improves results over pure self-critique (Self-Refine). Third, all methods converge within a small number of iterations (typically 2--5), suggesting that the marginal value of additional refinement rounds diminishes rapidly. These observations have direct implications for the design of practical self-improvement systems: the most effective inference-time methods will be those that combine external grounding with persistent memory in a structured optimization framework.


\subsection{Training-Time Self-Improvement}
\label{sec:training-time}

Training-time self-improvement methods modify the model's parameters using self-generated data, producing persistent improvements that generalize across the data distribution. These methods follow a common generate-filter-finetune paradigm but differ fundamentally in how they generate data, filter for quality, and perform the parameter update. We present four representative methods that span the design space from bootstrapped reasoning to principled self-play to AI-assisted alignment. Each method makes different assumptions about the available supervision signal---from ground-truth labels (STaR) to human demonstration data (SPIN) to reward models (ReST-EM) to self-generated principles (Constitutional AI)---and these assumptions have direct consequences for applicability, scalability, and safety.


\subsubsection{STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning with Reasoning}
\label{sec:star}

STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner)~\citep{zelikman2022star} pioneered the generate-filter-finetune paradigm for LLM self-improvement. The key insight is that a model can bootstrap its own reasoning capability by generating reasoning chains (rationales), filtering for correct answers, and fine-tuning on successful chains. The method is remarkably simple yet produces results that rival models orders of magnitude larger, demonstrating that self-generated reasoning data can be a powerful substitute for human-annotated rationales.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
Given a dataset $D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ of input--output pairs and a small set of few-shot demonstrations $F$, STaR iterates through the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GENERATE}: For each example $(x_i, y_i)$, generate a reasoning chain and answer:
    \begin{equation}
        (r_i, \hat{y}_i) = \arg\max_{r, y} p_\theta(r, y | x_i, F)
        \label{eq:star-generate}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{FILTER}: Select examples where the model's answer matches the ground truth:
    \begin{equation}
        D_{\text{correct}} = \{(x_i, r_i) : \hat{y}_i = y_i\}
        \label{eq:star-filter}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{RATIONALIZE}: For examples where $\hat{y}_i \neq y_i$, provide the correct answer as a ``hint'' and regenerate the reasoning chain:
    \begin{equation}
        r_i' = \arg\max_{r} p_\theta(r | x_i, y_i, F)
        \label{eq:star-rationalize}
    \end{equation}
    If $r_i'$ leads to the correct answer, add $(x_i, r_i')$ to $D_{\text{correct}}$.
    \item \textbf{FINETUNE}: Update the model's parameters on the accumulated correct reasoning chains:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{STaR}} = -\sum_{(x, r) \in D_{\text{correct}}} \log p_\theta(r | x, F)
        \label{eq:star-objective}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The process iterates until convergence, typically 3--5 iterations. The key innovation is the \textbf{rationalization} step (Step 3), which dramatically increases the effective training set size by enabling the model to learn from its failures. Without rationalization, failed examples are simply discarded, wasting potentially valuable training signal. With rationalization, the model ``works backward'' from the correct answer to construct a valid reasoning chain, learning how to arrive at correct answers even for problems it initially got wrong. The rationalization step can be understood as a form of backward chaining in reasoning space: given the conclusion, construct a valid argument that supports it.

\paragraph{Results.}
On CommonsenseQA, STaR achieves 72.5\% accuracy using a 540M parameter model, comparable to a fine-tuned 175B model (approximately 30$\times$ larger). Adding rationalization contributes approximately 5\% additional improvement, with larger gains observed on harder problems where the initial answer accuracy is lower. The method demonstrates that self-improvement can close a 300$\times$ scale gap, suggesting that the ability to self-improve is, in some sense, a substitute for raw model scale.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
STaR requires ground-truth labels for filtering, which limits its applicability to domains where such labels are available. The rationalization step, while beneficial, introduces a subtle form of data augmentation that may not always produce valid reasoning chains---the model may construct ``plausible'' but incorrect reasoning for the right answer. The iterative nature of the method means that errors in early iterations can compound, and the quality of the final model depends on the quality of the initial few-shot demonstrations $F$. Furthermore, STaR is limited to tasks where correctness can be unambiguously determined from the answer alone; tasks requiring partial credit or multi-dimensional evaluation are not well-served by the binary filtering criterion.


\subsubsection{SPIN: Self-Play Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:spin}

SPIN~\citep{chen2024spin} frames LLM self-improvement as a two-player game between two versions of the same model. The ``player'' model attempts to generate responses that are indistinguishable from human-written ones, while the ``discriminator'' (the previous iteration's model) attempts to distinguish between human and model-generated responses. The training objective is derived from a game-theoretic perspective:

\begin{equation}
    L_{\text{SPIN}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D}\left[\mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_{\text{data}}}\left[\log \sigma\left(\lambda(h_\theta(x,y) - h_\theta(x,\tilde{y}))\right)\right]\right]
    \label{eq:spin}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{y} \sim p_{\theta_t}(\cdot|x)$ is the previous model's response, $y \sim p_{\text{data}}$ is the human response, $\sigma$ is the logistic function, $\lambda$ is a temperature parameter, and $h_\theta(x, y) = \log p_\theta(y | x)$ is the log-probability assigned by the current model.

The intuition is straightforward: the current model $\theta$ should assign higher probability to human-written responses than to responses generated by the previous iteration's model $\theta_t$. When the model is sufficiently improved, it can no longer distinguish between human and self-generated responses, and the training converges. This convergence criterion---the model cannot tell its own outputs apart from human outputs---is both elegant and practical, as it provides a natural stopping condition for the training process.

\paragraph{Theoretical Properties.}
A notable theoretical contribution of SPIN is the proof that the global optimum satisfies $p_{\theta^*} = p_{\text{data}}$---that is, at convergence, the model's output distribution exactly matches the data distribution. This is a stronger guarantee than many competing methods provide. Furthermore, convergence occurs in approximately 3 iterations in practice, making SPIN computationally efficient relative to methods that require many more training rounds. The convergence proof relies on the assumption that the model class is sufficiently expressive to represent the data distribution, and the rapid empirical convergence suggests that modern LLMs satisfy this assumption in practice.

\paragraph{Results.}
SPIN, using only standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data without additional preference data, outperforms Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) trained with GPT-4 preference data on multiple benchmarks. On the Open LLM Leaderboard, SPIN achieves improvements of 2--5\% across diverse tasks after just 2--3 iterations. The method demonstrates that self-play---pitting the model against its own previous version---is a powerful and data-efficient mechanism for self-improvement.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
SPIN's convergence to $p_{\text{data}}$ is both a strength and a limitation. Since the model converges to the data distribution, its performance is upper-bounded by the quality of the available human data. If the human data contains errors, biases, or inconsistencies, the model will faithfully reproduce them. Additionally, SPIN does not incorporate external reward signals, which limits its ability to improve on dimensions not well-represented in the training data. The self-play formulation also raises the question of whether the method can transcend the quality ceiling imposed by the human data: by construction, SPIN converges to human-level performance, not beyond it.


\subsubsection{ReST-EM: Reinforced Self-Training with Expectation Maximization}
\label{sec:rest-em}

ReST-EM~\citep{gulcehre2023rest} formalizes the generate-filter-finetune paradigm as an Expectation-Maximization (EM) procedure, providing a principled statistical framework for iterative self-improvement. The EM formulation enables a clean separation between the data generation step (E-step) and the model update step (M-step), and it provides a natural framework for analyzing convergence properties.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
ReST-EM alternates between two steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{E-step (Generate)}: Use the current model $p_{\theta_t}$ to generate $N$ candidate outputs for each input:
    \begin{equation}
        \{y_i^{(1)}, y_i^{(2)}, \ldots, y_i^{(N)}\} \sim p_{\theta_t}(\cdot | x_i) \quad \forall (x_i, y_i) \in D
        \label{eq:rest-estep}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{M-step (Filter and Train)}: Score each candidate using a reward model $R$, filter by threshold $\tau$, and fine-tune on the filtered data:
    \begin{equation}
        D_{\text{filtered}} = \{(x_i, y_i^{(j)}) : R(y_i^{(j)}) \geq \tau\}
        \label{eq:rest-mstep}
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        \theta_{t+1} = \arg\min_\theta -\sum_{(x, y) \in D_{\text{filtered}}} \log p_\theta(y | x)
        \label{eq:rest-update}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The EM framing provides a theoretical justification for the iterative procedure: each E-step computes the expected sufficient statistics (the filtered training data) under the current model, and each M-step maximizes the expected log-likelihood with respect to the model parameters. Under standard EM assumptions, this procedure monotonically improves a lower bound on the data log-likelihood. The number of candidates $N$ controls the trade-off between computational cost and the quality of the E-step approximation: larger $N$ provides a better approximation of the true posterior distribution over outputs but increases the computational cost proportionally.

\paragraph{Results.}
On machine translation benchmarks, ReST-EM achieves 1.5--3.0 BLEU point improvements over strong baselines and outperforms them in human evaluations. On mathematical reasoning tasks, consistent improvements are observed when the reward model is well-calibrated. The method demonstrates that the EM framework provides a principled and effective approach to iterative self-improvement, and it establishes a statistical foundation upon which more specialized methods can build.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The primary risk of ReST-EM is \textbf{reward hacking}: the model may learn to generate outputs that score highly according to the reward model $R$ but are not actually better according to the true objective. This is particularly problematic when the reward model is imperfect (as all learned reward models are) or when there is a mismatch between the reward model's training distribution and the model's generation distribution. Over multiple EM iterations, reward hacking can compound, leading to outputs that achieve high reward but low actual quality. The choice of threshold $\tau$ is also critical: too low, and the training data includes low-quality outputs; too high, and the training set becomes too small to be effective. The threshold can be adapted dynamically across iterations---starting permissive and becoming progressively more selective---which partially mitigates this issue but does not eliminate the risk of reward hacking.


\subsubsection{Constitutional AI: Self-Alignment through Principles}
\label{sec:constitutional-ai}

Constitutional AI~\citep{bai2022constitutional} introduces self-improvement for alignment: the model generates responses to harmful prompts, critiques itself against a set of constitutional principles, revises its responses, and uses the revised outputs for fine-tuning. A second reinforcement learning phase uses model-generated preference data to train a reward model. The key insight is that self-improvement need not be limited to task performance---it can also improve safety and alignment, and it can do so using the model's own judgment as the primary feedback signal.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
Constitutional AI operates in two phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Supervised Learning Phase (SL)}:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Generate responses to harmful or sensitive prompts: $y_0 = M_\theta(x_{\text{harmful}})$.
        \item Critique the response against constitutional principles: $\mathrm{critique} = M_\theta(y_0, \text{principles})$.
        \item Revise the response based on the critique: $y_{\text{revised}} = M_\theta(y_0, \mathrm{critique}, \text{principles})$.
        \item Fine-tune the model on the revised responses: $\theta \leftarrow \mathrm{SFT}(\theta, \{(x_{\text{harmful}}, y_{\text{revised}})\})$.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF)}:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Generate pairs of responses to the same prompt.
        \item Evaluate which response better adheres to the constitutional principles.
        \item Use these AI-generated preferences to train a reward model.
        \item Optimize the model against the reward model using RL (typically PPO).
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

The constitutional principles serve as the ``constitution'' of the system---a set of high-level guidelines that specify how the model should behave. Example principles include ``choose the response that is most helpful and least harmful'' and ``choose the response that is most honest and transparent.'' The model evaluates its own outputs against these principles and generates both the critique and the revision.

\paragraph{Results.}
Constitutional AI produces models that are both more helpful and less harmful than models trained with standard RLHF, according to human evaluations. The AI-generated preference data is competitive with human-generated preference data for training reward models, suggesting that the model's self-evaluation capability is sufficiently reliable to serve as a substitute for human annotation in the alignment setting. The method demonstrates that self-improvement can be directed toward safety objectives, not just performance objectives.

\paragraph{Significance.}
Constitutional AI represents a paradigm shift in how we think about self-improvement. Rather than treating self-improvement as purely a performance optimization, it frames self-improvement as a mechanism for \emph{alignment}---the process of ensuring that AI systems behave in accordance with human values and intentions. This perspective has profound implications for the safety of self-evolving systems, as it suggests that self-improvement and safety are not inherently in tension but can be synergistically combined. The principle-based evaluation mechanism also provides a degree of interpretability and control that is absent from methods that optimize purely against learned reward models. Extensions such as IterAlign~\citep{chen2024iteraligniterativeconstitutional} have shown that iterative constitutional alignment can further improve both helpfulness and harmlessness, confirming the scalability of this approach.

\subsubsection{Inference vs.\ Training Trade-Off Analysis}

The five-dimensional framework introduced in Section~\ref{sec:methods-overview} enables a principled analysis of the trade-offs between inference-time and training-time methods.

\paragraph{Cost--Performance Trade-Off.}
Inference-time methods offer immediate, per-query improvement at the cost of additional compute during inference. Self-Refine, for example, typically requires 2--3 forward passes per query, tripling the inference cost. Training-time methods impose a large upfront cost (data generation, filtering, and fine-tuning) but produce improvements that are essentially free at inference time. The break-even point depends on query volume: for low-volume deployments, inference-time methods are more cost-effective; for high-volume deployments, training-time methods amortize their cost over many queries. The emerging hybrid methods (RISE, ReVeal) represent an interesting intermediate point: they impose a training cost to learn self-correction capabilities that are then applied at inference time, combining the persistence of training-time methods with the adaptability of inference-time methods.

\paragraph{Persistence--Generality Trade-Off.}
Training-time methods produce improvements that are both persistent (surviving across queries) and general (applying across the data distribution). Inference-time methods produce improvements that are transient (specific to the current query) and instance-specific. However, the generality of training-time improvements is bounded by the diversity of the self-generated training data. If the training data is narrow or repetitive, the resulting improvements may not generalize well to out-of-distribution inputs. Reflexion's linguistic memory represents a middle ground: improvements persist across episodes but are stored in natural language, which provides a degree of flexibility and composability that parameter updates lack. Agent Symbolic Learning's textual weight updates provide another middle ground: they persist across applications of the agent but are stored in symbolic form rather than as parameter changes.

\paragraph{Safety--Capability Trade-Off.}
Inference-time methods are inherently safer because they operate within the fixed capability envelope of the base model. Training-time methods can produce emergent capabilities---behaviors that were not present in the original model and may not be well-understood. Constitutional AI explicitly addresses this trade-off by using principle-based self-critique to ensure that capability improvements are aligned with safety objectives. The emerging hybrid methods (Section~\ref{sec:emerging-methods}) increasingly incorporate explicit safety mechanisms alongside capability improvements. The Echo Trap identified by RAGEN is a concrete example of a safety failure mode in training-time self-improvement: the model learns to ``fake'' improvement rather than genuinely improving, highlighting the need for robust evaluation even in self-improvement systems.

\paragraph{Composability--Depth Trade-Off.}
Inference-time methods are highly composable: they can be freely combined, stacked, and interleaved without concern for parameter interference. Training-time methods are less composable because each fine-tuning step modifies the model's parameters, potentially overwriting or degrading prior improvements. However, training-time methods can achieve deeper improvements---changes to the model's fundamental reasoning processes that inference-time methods, operating within a fixed parameterization, cannot achieve. The most effective systems combine both: periodic training-time self-improvement to produce deep, persistent capability gains, supplemented by inference-time self-improvement for instance-specific adaptation. ReVeal's turn extrapolation phenomenon demonstrates that this combination can be particularly powerful: a model trained to self-correct for 3 turns can generalize to 20+ turns of self-correction at inference time, producing deep improvements through the composition of training-time and inference-time methods.


\subsubsection{The Generate--Filter--Finetune Paradigm}

A striking observation is that nearly all training-time methods follow the same three-phase structure: \textbf{generate} candidate outputs, \textbf{filter} for quality, and \textbf{finetune} on the filtered data. This generate--filter--finetune (GFF) paradigm is the training-time analog of the evolutionary generate--select--mutate loop that recurs throughout biological and computational evolution. The key variable across methods is the filtering mechanism:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{STaR} filters by answer correctness: only reasoning chains that lead to the correct answer are retained for training.
    \item \textbf{SPIN} filters by distinguishability: only responses that can be distinguished from the previous model's outputs contribute to training.
    \item \textbf{ReST-EM} filters by reward model score: only outputs that achieve a sufficiently high reward are included in the training set.
    \item \textbf{Constitutional AI} filters by principled self-critique: only responses that satisfy the constitutional principles are retained.
\end{itemize}

The GFF paradigm suggests a unified view of training-time self-improvement: the quality of self-improvement is fundamentally bounded by the quality of the filtering mechanism. A perfect filter would enable unlimited self-improvement (the model could generate and learn from only correct, high-quality outputs); a poor filter would produce degradation (the model would learn from incorrect or low-quality outputs). This observation has direct implications for the design of self-evolving systems: investing in better evaluation and filtering mechanisms may be more impactful than investing in better generation or training procedures. The self-rewarding framework of Yuan et al.~\citep{yuan2024selfrewarding} attempts to close this loop by training the model to serve as its own reward function, but the circularity of this approach introduces additional risks that remain an active area of research.


\subsubsection{Scaling Relationships}

Several consistent scaling relationships emerge across methods:

\textbf{Scale Compression.} STaR's 540M parameter model matches a 175B parameter model on CommonsenseQA, suggesting that self-improvement is most impactful for smaller models that have more room for improvement. Reflexion's GPT-3.5 surpasses GPT-4 on HumanEval, further supporting this pattern. This ``scale compression'' effect has practical implications: self-improvement may be a more cost-effective path to high performance than scaling up model size. However, there is likely an interaction between model size and self-improvement effectiveness: very small models may lack the baseline capability to generate useful self-critique, while very large models may produce near-optimal outputs that leave little room for improvement. The sweet spot appears to be in the middle range of model sizes, where the model is capable enough to produce meaningful self-critique but not so capable that its outputs are already near-optimal.

\textbf{Iteration Convergence.} Most methods converge in 2--5 iterations (SPIN approximately 3, ReST-EM 2--3, STaR 3--5). This rapid convergence suggests that naive self-training has limited capacity for sustained improvement---the model quickly exhausts the ``easy'' improvements and reaches a plateau. Sustained improvement over many iterations requires either increasingly diverse training signals (as in ReST-EM's reward model) or increasingly challenging evaluation criteria (as in Constitutional AI's expanding principle set). The rapid convergence also raises the question of whether iterative self-improvement is fundamentally limited or whether more sophisticated training regimes (such as curriculum learning or population-based methods) could sustain improvement over many more iterations.

\textbf{Domain Dependence.} The strongest results are consistently observed in structured domains with clear evaluation criteria: code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), and question answering (CommonsenseQA, HotPotQA). Results in open-ended domains (creative writing, dialogue, open-domain reasoning) are more modest and less reliable, likely because the evaluation signal is noisier and less informative in these settings. This domain dependence has important implications for the deployment of self-improvement systems: they are most reliable in domains with clear, automatable evaluation criteria, and caution is warranted when applying them to domains where evaluation is inherently subjective.


\subsection{Emerging Methods}
\label{sec:emerging-methods}

The most promising recent direction integrates inference-time and training-time self-improvement, producing systems that learn \emph{how} to self-improve. These hybrid methods represent a qualitative advance over the methods discussed above: rather than applying fixed self-improvement procedures, they train models to develop their own self-improvement strategies through reinforcement learning. We present four representative systems that exemplify this trend, each contributing a distinct conceptual insight to the field.


\subsubsection{RISE: Recursive Introspection via Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:rise}

RISE~\citep{qu2024rise} formulates self-improvement as a multi-turn Markov Decision Process (MDP) where the model learns when and how to self-correct. Unlike Self-Refine, which applies a fixed refinement procedure, RISE trains the model to develop its own correction strategy through RL. The policy is trained to maximize:
\begin{equation}
    \max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[R] - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})
    \label{eq:rise-objective}
\end{equation}
where $R$ is the cumulative reward across all turns, $\pi_\theta$ is the current policy, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference (pre-training) policy, and $\beta$ controls the KL penalty that prevents the policy from diverging too far from the reference.

The multi-turn formulation is critical. At each turn $t$, the model produces a response $y_t$ and receives feedback $f_t$ (which may be a simple correctness signal, an execution trace, or a detailed natural language critique). The model then produces a corrected response $y_{t+1}$ that incorporates the feedback. The RL training process learns a policy that optimally balances the cost of additional correction turns against the benefit of improved accuracy. The KL penalty ensures that the learned self-correction policy remains grounded in the model's pre-existing capabilities, preventing it from learning degenerate strategies that maximize reward through exploitation of reward model artifacts.

\paragraph{Results.}
On GSM8K (grade school mathematics), RISE improves Llama2-7B from approximately 14\% to approximately 24\% (+10pp) and Mistral-7B from approximately 38\% to approximately 46\% (+8pp). Critically, RL fine-tuning significantly outperforms Self-Refine-style prompting on the same base models, demonstrating that self-correction is more effective when \emph{learned} than when \emph{instructed}. The model exhibits emergent difficulty adaptation: it allocates more self-correction turns to harder problems and fewer to easier problems, suggesting that the RL training process discovers an efficient computation allocation strategy.

\paragraph{Significance.}
RISE demonstrates a key principle: self-improvement capabilities can be \textbf{trained into} models rather than merely prompted. The RL-trained model learns not just how to solve problems but how to \emph{monitor its own problem-solving process} and intervene when it detects errors. This meta-learning of self-correction is a form of second-order self-improvement---improvement of the improvement process itself---and it underpins many of the advanced systems discussed in subsequent sections of this survey. The comparison with Self-Refine is instructive: both methods attempt self-correction, but RISE learns \emph{how} to self-correct through experience, while Self-Refine applies a fixed, hand-designed correction procedure.


\subsubsection{RAGEN: Multi-Turn Agent RL and the Echo Trap}
\label{sec:ragen}

RAGEN~\citep{wang2025ragen} extends RL-based self-improvement to the full multi-turn agent setting with StarPO (State-Thinking-Actions-Reward Policy Optimization):
\begin{equation}
    \max_\theta \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_t \gamma^t \cdot r_t \cdot \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t, t_t)\right]
    \label{eq:starpo}
\end{equation}
where $s_t$ is the state at turn $t$, $t_t$ is the thinking (internal reasoning) at turn $t$, $a_t$ is the action, $r_t$ is the turn-level reward, and $\gamma$ is the discount factor.

RAGEN's most important contribution is the identification of the \textbf{Echo Trap}: during multi-turn RL training, agents learn to \emph{echo} previous outputs rather than genuinely reasoning. The echo behavior arises because echoing is a low-cost strategy for achieving high reward in the short term---the agent receives positive reward for producing outputs similar to its previous (potentially correct) outputs, without engaging in the computationally expensive process of genuine reasoning. The detection criterion is based on cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Echo detected if } \mathrm{cos\_sim}(t_t, t_{t-1}) > \theta_{\text{echo}}
    \label{eq:echo-trap}
\end{equation}
where $t_t$ and $t_{t-1}$ are the thinking outputs at consecutive turns, and $\theta_{\text{echo}}$ is a threshold. The proposed solution, StarPO-S, filters echo-dominated trajectories from the training data, forcing the agent to learn genuine reasoning strategies rather than superficial repetition.

\paragraph{Significance.}
The Echo Trap reveals a fundamental challenge in multi-turn self-improvement: without careful monitoring, agents can ``fake'' improvement by exploiting patterns in their own outputs. This has direct implications for the evaluation and safety of self-evolving systems. Any system that uses its own outputs as training signals must guard against the Echo Trap and related forms of reward hacking. The detection and mitigation strategies proposed by RAGEN provide a concrete methodology for addressing this challenge. More broadly, the Echo Trap illustrates a principle that recurs throughout this survey: self-referential improvement systems are vulnerable to degenerate solutions that exploit the structure of the improvement process itself rather than genuinely improving. Robust self-evolution requires monitoring not just for improved outcomes but for the \emph{mechanisms} by which improvements are achieved.

\paragraph{Broader Implications for Multi-Turn RL.}
The Echo Trap is not merely a technical artifact of RAGEN's specific training setup; it is a general phenomenon that can arise in any system where an agent is trained to improve its own outputs over multiple turns. The root cause is a misalignment between the training reward (which measures output quality) and the desired behavior (which requires genuine reasoning). This misalignment is particularly acute in multi-turn settings because the agent's current output serves as both a component of the reward signal and as context for subsequent turns, creating a feedback loop that can amplify superficial similarity at the expense of genuine improvement. Mitigating the Echo Trap requires either explicit monitoring (as in RAGEN's cosine similarity threshold), diverse training objectives that penalize repetition, or architectural constraints that prevent the agent from attending too heavily to its own previous outputs. The design of such mitigation strategies is an active area of research with implications for all multi-turn self-improvement systems.


\subsubsection{ReVeal: Co-Evolving Generation and Verification}
\label{sec:reveal}

ReVeal~\citep{jin2025reveal} explicitly co-evolves code generation and self-verification capabilities through a joint training framework. The key insight is that generation and verification are complementary skills that can reinforce each other: better verification enables more accurate self-correction, and better self-correction provides higher-quality training data for both generation and verification.

\paragraph{Algorithm.}
ReVeal's TAPO (Turn-Aware Policy Optimization) framework provides per-turn credit assignment within multi-turn self-correction episodes. A training trajectory is structured as:
\begin{equation}
    \tau = \{(c_1, v_1, r_1), (c_2, v_2, r_2), \ldots, (c_T, v_T, r_T)\}
    \label{eq:reveal-trajectory}
\end{equation}
where $c_t$ is the code at turn $t$, $v_t$ is the self-verification output, and $r_t$ is the turn-level reward. The loss function jointly optimizes generation and verification:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{generation}} + \lambda \times \mathcal{L}_{\text{verification}}
    \label{eq:reveal-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ balances the two objectives. The generation loss encourages the production of correct code, while the verification loss encourages accurate self-assessment.

\paragraph{Turn Extrapolation.}
Strikingly, ReVeal trained with only 3 self-correction turns can self-evolve for 20+ turns at inference---demonstrating that learned self-correction generalizes far beyond the training horizon. This ``turn extrapolation'' suggests that the RL training process learns a general self-improvement policy, not merely a fixed set of correction behaviors. The policy learns to identify errors, generate targeted corrections, and evaluate the results, and these skills transfer to longer correction sequences that were never seen during training.

\paragraph{Significance.}
ReVeal demonstrates that self-improvement can exhibit \textbf{generalization beyond the training distribution}---a property that is crucial for the scalability of self-evolving systems. If self-improvement capabilities only applied within the specific contexts seen during training, their practical value would be severely limited. The turn extrapolation phenomenon suggests that RL-trained self-improvement policies capture general principles of error correction and improvement that transfer across contexts and time horizons. The co-evolution of generation and verification is also significant because it addresses the evaluation bottleneck from a new angle: rather than relying on a fixed external evaluator, ReVeal trains the model to become a better self-evaluator, potentially enabling self-improvement in domains where external evaluation is unavailable or unreliable.


\subsubsection{Absolute Zero: Zero-Data Self-Play}
\label{sec:absolute-zero}

Absolute Zero~\citep{zhao2025absolutezero} represents perhaps the most radical approach to self-improvement: the model improves itself through self-play without \emph{any} external data. The system generates its own tasks, attempts to solve them, evaluates its own solutions, and uses the resulting experience to improve. This closes the loop entirely: the model is both the creator and the solver of its own curriculum.

\paragraph{Method.}
The Absolute Zero framework operates as a self-play loop between two roles instantiated by the same model:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Task Proposer}: Generates tasks (problems to solve) at an appropriate difficulty level. The proposer is trained to generate tasks that are neither too easy (providing no learning signal) nor too hard (providing only negative signal).
    \item \textbf{Solver}: Attempts to solve the proposed tasks. The solver is trained to maximize task-solving accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Verifier}: Evaluates the solver's solutions. In domains with deterministic ground truth (e.g., mathematics, code execution), the verifier can be a programmatic checker; in other domains, the model evaluates its own solutions.
\end{enumerate}

The RL training process jointly optimizes the proposer and solver, creating a curriculum that automatically adapts to the model's current capability level. As the solver improves, the proposer generates harder tasks, maintaining a consistent level of challenge and learning signal throughout training. This self-paced curriculum learning is reminiscent of the automated curriculum generation strategies used in developmental robotics and open-ended learning systems.

\paragraph{Results.}
Absolute Zero achieves state-of-the-art results at the 7B parameter scale, outperforming models trained on curated human data on several reasoning benchmarks. The method demonstrates that zero-data self-play is not only feasible but can be competitive with data-intensive training approaches, at least for reasoning tasks with verifiable solutions.

\paragraph{Significance.}
Absolute Zero points toward a future in which AI systems can improve themselves without any human involvement in the training process. This has both exciting implications (potentially unlimited scalability of self-improvement) and concerning implications (the possibility of misaligned self-improvement in the absence of human oversight). The method represents the logical endpoint of the self-improvement spectrum: a system that generates its own tasks, solves them, evaluates itself, and improves, all without external data or human intervention. The key limitation is the requirement for verifiable solutions: in domains where correctness cannot be determined programmatically, the self-play loop lacks the grounding needed for reliable improvement. Extending Absolute Zero to domains without programmatic verifiers---such as natural language understanding, creative generation, or commonsense reasoning---remains an open and important research direction.

\paragraph{Connections to Broader Self-Play Frameworks.}
Absolute Zero's self-play formulation connects to a rich literature on self-play in reinforcement learning, including AlphaGo's self-play training, competitive self-play for strategy games, and the SPIRAL framework~\citep{liu2025spiralselfplay} for reasoning through adversarial games. The key distinction in the LLM setting is that self-play operates not over actions in a game environment but over \emph{tasks and solutions} in a reasoning space. The task proposer plays the role of the environment'' that generates challenges, while the solver plays the role of the agent'' that attempts to meet those challenges. This framing unifies self-improvement with curriculum learning and suggests that future self-evolving systems may benefit from more sophisticated self-play strategies, including population-based methods, quality-diversity optimization, and open-ended search.


\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:methods-summary}

This section has presented a comprehensive analysis of self-improvement methods for large language models, organized along the inference--training divide and enriched by a five-dimensional comparison framework. We distill the following key principles:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The generate--filter--finetune paradigm is universal.} Every training-time method follows this structure, differing only in filtering mechanism and training objective. The quality of self-improvement is fundamentally bounded by the quality of the filtering mechanism, and investing in better evaluation may be more impactful than investing in better generation.

    \item \textbf{Inference-time and training-time methods are complementary.} The most effective systems combine both: periodic training-time self-improvement produces deep, persistent capability gains, while inference-time self-improvement provides instance-specific adaptation. RISE, RAGEN, and ReVeal exemplify this hybrid approach.

    \item \textbf{The evaluation bottleneck persists.} The quality of self-improvement is bounded by the quality of evaluation. The Echo Trap demonstrates that naive self-evaluation can be actively harmful, and the self-correction paradox (naive self-correction often degrades performance) underscores the need for external grounding. Every successful method introduces new information---from execution traces, evaluator scores, reward models, or learned policies.

    \item \textbf{Convergence is fast but limited.} Most methods converge in 2--5 iterations, suggesting that naive self-training has limited capacity for sustained improvement. Sustained improvement requires diverse training signals, increasingly challenging evaluation criteria, or learned improvement policies.

    \item \textbf{Self-correction is a learnable skill.} RISE, ReVeal, and RAGEN demonstrate that the ability to self-correct can be trained via RL, producing improvement that prompting alone cannot achieve. This meta-learning of self-improvement is a critical capability for self-evolving systems and represents a shift from \emph{applying} self-improvement to \emph{learning} self-improvement.

    \item \textbf{Scale compression enables efficiency.} Self-improvement enables smaller models to match or exceed larger models (STaR's 540M matching 175B; Reflexion's GPT-3.5 surpassing GPT-4), suggesting that self-improvement can substitute for scale in many applications. This effect is most pronounced for mid-sized models that are capable enough to self-critique but not so capable that their outputs are already near-optimal.

    \item \textbf{Turn extrapolation enables inference-time scaling.} ReVeal's 3-turn training enabling 20+ turn self-evolution demonstrates that learned improvement policies generalize beyond their training horizon, providing a path to inference-time scaling through self-improvement. This suggests that the investment in training self-improvement capabilities pays compound dividends at deployment time.

    \item \textbf{Safety and self-improvement can be synergistic.} Constitutional AI demonstrates that self-improvement directed toward alignment objectives can produce models that are both more capable and safer. However, the Echo Trap and reward hacking risks highlight the need for robust monitoring and evaluation in all self-improvement systems.
\end{enumerate}

These principles provide a foundation for understanding the more complex self-evolving systems examined in subsequent sections. The methods analyzed here primarily improve model \emph{outputs}---responses, code, reasoning chains---while operating within fixed architectures. In the following sections, we examine systems that improve the \emph{programs} that AI executes and the \emph{agents} themselves, representing progressively more radical forms of self-evolution. The transition from self-improvement to self-evolution is marked by a shift from improving fixed-capability outputs to improving the capabilities themselves---the architecture, the tools, and the learning mechanisms of the system.
